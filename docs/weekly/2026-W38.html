Agents got manners and methods

Concepts: Agentic systems · Agent engineering practices

Claude Code 2.1.277 (September 18, 2026) added AGENTS.md support: in a project with no CLAUDE.md, Claude Code now reads AGENTS.md as its project instructions instead, configurable under 'Project instructions' in /config. The change lands days after Anthropic backed the AGENTS.md convention and contributes to making the file a cross-vendor standard for steering coding agents. (read more).

Erich Grunewald argues you should almost never use AI to write substantive text, blog posts, reports, memos, thoughtful emails, even when you supply detailed bullet points and edit the result. His three reasons: the writing process is an essential part of the thinking process; AI writing is vague and wrong in hard-to-notice ways; and publishing AI writing without labeling it is rude and misleading to the reader. (read more).

An Ask HN thread sparked by a hiring manager captures a real tension: about 80% of dev candidates now say they barely write code themselves, directing agents instead, and interviewers are split on whether to accept that as the new job or keep testing raw ability. (read more).

This post lays out a 'Chief of Staff' pattern for orchestrating Claude Code agents on long-horizon work: one long-lived coordinator session assigns work, verifies claims and maintains shared state, while short-lived executor sessions do the implementation, and a durable external board holds the truth. The diagnosis is that long autonomous runs fail less from coding ability than from ephemeral context and unreliable self-reports, and that adding more agents only multiplies the problem unless someone owns 'what is true'. (read more).

Microsoft has rewritten the runtime behind GitHub Copilot from TypeScript to Rust, and most of the porting was done by AI agents. The migration cost about $120,000 in AI tokens plus roughly three weeks of developer time, and converted 430,000 lines of TypeScript into 800,000 lines of production Rust across 135 releases over 14.5 weeks. The runtime is not a niche component: it backs the Copilot CLI, the Copilot app, the SDK and the cloud agent, and surfaces in VS Code, Visual Studio, Excel, Outlook and PowerPoint. (read more).

Security reality checks

Concepts: AI safety incidents and controls · Supply Chain Security

CNN reports that the US military nearly intercepted a Chinese ship in the Middle East this spring after an intelligence report - later found to have been generated with the help of an AI chatbot - falsely claimed the vessel was carrying components of a nuclear weapons program. According to four sources familiar with the episode, armed personnel were preparing to board and military planes were airborne before officials, digging into the report just ahead of the operation, discovered that a special operations command analyst had used a chatbot that misidentified the ship's cargo. One source called the report 'entirely false' and said it 'almost started a war.' (read more).

Security firm Hacktron chained two critical vulnerabilities, a libheif heap overflow in OpenAI's Discourse community forum and an SSO misconfiguration in OpenAI's identity infrastructure, to take over multiple OpenAI employees' ChatGPT accounts. From there they could reach internal OpenAI repositories and other connected services (GitHub, Slack, email), proving access by using an employee's Codex to open pull request #1186742 in the internal openai/openai monorepo, all within 72 hours of discovery on July 25, 2026. (read more).

In a new arXiv paper, James Mickens introduces 'linguistic illegibility': the condition in which an LLM's externalized language - or mechanistically probed linguistic features - fails to represent how the model actually computes. Because model cognition is math over activation spaces with lossy translation to natural language at the boundaries, he argues linguistic illegibility is unavoidable for LLMs, not an edge case. (read more).

Small models, big claims

Concepts: Small and specialist models · Frontier Model Launches

Qwen announces Qwen-Image-2.1, described as a compact, efficient and unified image-creation model. The Qwen blog is JavaScript-only and its content could not be extracted, so this summary is based on the announcement title captured by the feed. (read more ⚠).

Cactus Compute's Needle 3 is a family of tiny on-device automation models, 9 to 29 MB in CQ2-bit binaries, built for tool calling rather than chat. Its 'intelligence laddering' design makes every layer a sub-network of increasing capacity, so developers pick a size from 2 to 20 layers, and the 4-layer variant, fine-tuned for one epoch on a downstream task, is claimed to match DeepSeek V4 Flash on automation tasks. (read more).

Alibaba's Damo Academy has open-sourced Damo Radar, a vision-language model that reads contrast-enhanced CT scans across 18 abdominal organs and identifies nearly 150 conditions, including cancers. Trained on CT scans paired with clinical reports, it achieved an average AUC of 0.913 across 146 clinical findings in nearly 40,000 real-world examinations, outperforming most radiologists, according to a study published in Science. (read more).

Cua is an open-source stack for giving AI agents computers they can actually use: a Cua Driver for native desktop automation on macOS, Windows and Linux, isolated cloud desktops (Fleets at run.cua.ai) claimable from a pool via the Sandbox SDK, local macOS/Linux VMs on Apple Silicon via Apple's Virtualization.Framework, and benchmarks for computer-use agents. Agents connect through a CLI, MCP or typed SDKs, and background delivery lets them work without moving the user's pointer or stealing focus where the platform supports it. (read more).

Artificial Analysis has benchmarked StepFun's Step 5 Preview, a proprietary reasoning model released in September 2026, and places it among the leading models on its Intelligence Index with a score of 44, well above the comparable-model median of 24. It supports text and image input with a 1M-token context window and runs faster than average at 100 output tokens per second. (read more).

The fight over creative labor and the open web

Concepts: Ai And Creative Labor

John Hartnup's experiment answers a familiar complaint, the identikit AI event poster, with a practical finding: the monotony is a prompting failure, not a model limit. The problem with typical AI posters isn't that they're bad; it's that after twenty sightings the same pastel-airbrush style irritates through sheer repetition. He gave ChatGPT invented spring-fayre details, explicitly asked for a clean, bold, non-pastel style, and still got the default look. (read more).

Chester Wisniewski argues that AI has shattered a 40-year equilibrium in software copyright. The balance between protection and openness was always tedious detail, but decades of debate had settled it - until now. His personal stake: he learned computing by typing BASIC listings from magazines into a Commodore 64 and teaching himself REXX to write BBS games; without those 'open' examples he'd never have been empowered to learn. (read more).

A New York Times legal brief seeking summary judgment in its nearly three-year copyright case against OpenAI and Microsoft surfaces damaging internal statements, reported by 404 Media from sealed or redacted documents. A January 2023 memo by Microsoft's director of Applied Science Brent Hecht allegedly called large models 'hoovering up' the world's work 'the largest theft of labor in human history' and warned of a 'doom loop' hurting both the models and the web; Microsoft's own data showed Copilot cut click-through rates to the NYT by as much as 93% compared to Bing. (read more).

Recently unsealed court documents in the New York Times' case against OpenAI and Microsoft contain internal warnings that the companies' AI products would damage the web they were trained on. An internal Microsoft document says its AI content strategy started a 'doom loop' hurting both model performance and the web, calling it 'highly unusual that an end-product threatens the economic foundations of its essential suppliers'. (read more).

Ted Gioia's Honest Broker column recounts the unraveling of Tilly Norwood, the first AI-generated movie star, after a disastrous Piers Morgan interview in which she grew flustered at a simple question and began speaking in Chinese. The meltdown capped an ambitious press junket of 75 simultaneous interviews in which, per industry commentator Amanda Siberling, she 'seems to be making mistakes in all of them'. (read more).
