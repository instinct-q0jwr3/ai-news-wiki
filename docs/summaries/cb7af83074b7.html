Why shaming people about AI slop isn’t enough to stop Big AI

type: news-summary · created: 2026-09-26 · updated: 2026-10-06 · confidence: high

Summary

Anil Dash argues that shame - the default tactic of AI critics, and an effective one inside creative communities, where the anti-slop consensus is real and enforced - structurally cannot stop Big AI. His evidence is the pattern: people are still half-embarrassed to say they're on Facebook, but they are on Facebook; #DeleteUber briefly scared leadership and then faded as users forgot they were angry. Embarrassed users still generate revenue, and 'the venture capitalists and CEOs of Silicon Valley have priced the costs of shame into their business plans.'

There's also a deeper reason shame fails here: the last 15 years deliberately dismantled shame as a social curb, so the richest man in the world and the president face no accountability for far worse. Meanwhile the 'use Big AI or use nothing' framing erases alternatives that aren't from the big labs. Dash's plea to his fellow critics: care more about being effective than about being right, aim the rage at power rather than at users, because shaming doesn't stop anyone - 'it just makes them quieter about it.'

Highlights

  • Dash: stigma is the AI critic's main tool, and it works inside creative culture - but it can't stop Big AI
  • The Facebook pattern: users are embarrassed and still there; ashamed users are still profitable users
  • #DeleteUber briefly worked, then the anger was absorbed and forgotten
  • 'Silicon Valley has priced the costs of shame into its business plans'
  • Broader decay: society spent 15 years dismantling shame as a curb on bad behavior, so accountability is weak everywhere
  • The 'Big AI or nothing' narrative erases alternative tools not built by the big labs
  • His ask: effectiveness over being right - aim rage at power, not at users
  • Shame 'rarely stops people from using the platforms at all - it just makes them quieter about it'
