How AI is an inherently imperial technology, and how the US and China are building rival "AI empires" with different approaches to extend their power

type: news-summary · created: 2026-10-22 · updated: 2026-10-30 · confidence: high

Summary

In a Foreign Affairs essay, Niall Ferguson, Lisa Klaassen, and Eric Schmidt argue that AI is an inherently imperial technology: by controlling the models, chips, infrastructure, and capital behind advanced machine cognition, the United States and China have each assembled a new kind of empire whose dominion extends over knowledge itself. Even without coercion or alliance building, every other country seeking access to advanced AI becomes beholden to one of the two.

The historical warning, they argue, is that empires rarely fall to challengers; they collapse when defending vast dominions drains them dry - an empire is only as durable as its capacity to pay for itself. The piece raises the prospect that China's one-party state may be building the instruments of its own demise, and frames the US-China contest as a rivalry between two AI empires setting the terms of future scientific and intellectual progress, with everyone else's sovereignty eroded by dependence.

Highlights

  • Ferguson, Klaassen, and Schmidt: AI is inherently imperial, and the US and China are building rival 'AI empires'
  • Control of models, chips, infrastructure, and capital amounts to dominion over knowledge itself
  • Other countries become beholden to a superpower simply by needing access to advanced AI
  • Historical pattern: empires collapse from the cost of defending their dominions, not from challengers
  • The authors suggest China's one-party state may be building the instruments of its own demise
  • 'AI superpowers' understates the reach and influence the two rivals now wield
