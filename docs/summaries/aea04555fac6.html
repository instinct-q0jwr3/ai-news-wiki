Pluralistic: The technopolitics of ad-tech (19 Oct 2026)

type: news-summary · created: 2026-10-19 · updated: 2026-10-28 · confidence: high

Summary

Cory Doctorow argues that the internet's original sin is not advertising but surveillance. Working from Langdon Winner's 'Do Artifacts Have Politics?', he distinguishes politics designed into artifacts (Robert Moses's bus-blocking bridges), politics that are accidental but unavoidable (the tomato harvester), and politics that are simply political choices - and puts ad-tech spying in the last category. Advertising itself, he says, is fine and even necessary: without channels for reaching unfamiliar audiences, political causes, books, music and community events would all suffer. The fusion of advertising with surveillance was a decision, not a technological inevitability - specifically the decision by US politicians to stop passing and enforcing privacy laws, leaving the Video Privacy Protection Act of VHS fame as the last consumer privacy statute. The downstream costs he catalogs include surveillance pricing, surveillance wages, dossiers feeding ICE deportation operations, and oligarchic power that finds targeted repression cheaper than broad prosperity. His conclusion: an internet compatible with privacy is possible because the surveillance was chosen and can be unchosen.

Highlights

  • Builds on Langdon Winner's framework of politics embedded in artifacts
  • Advertising per se is defensible - it lets new ideas, books and causes find audiences

  • The last US consumer privacy law was the 1988 Video Privacy Protection Act
  • Downstream harms cited: surveillance pricing, surveillance wages, ICE dossiers, oligarchy
