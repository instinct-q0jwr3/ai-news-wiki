How Alex Bores' Who Decides seeks to make AI safety a standard Democratic position, like being pro-choice, in 2028, as tech-funded rivals vie for influence

type: news-summary · created: 2026-11-03 · updated: 2026-11-03 · confidence: high

Summary

New York Magazine profiles the most unlikely power broker in Democratic AI politics: Alex Bores, a two-term New York state assemblyman who lost his June congressional primary - and turned that loss into a national platform. Bores sponsored New York's RAISE Act, which from January 1 requires AI companies to publish safety protocols and report catastrophic-risk incidents. His primary campaign for federal regulation drew roughly $8 million in opposition spending from Leading the Future, a super-PAC funded by an OpenAI executive; the attack made him a martyr, pulled in $18 million from pro-regulation tech figures, and earned him the Ezra Klein treatment. He lost to Micah Lasher anyway - and became, in NYMag's phrase, perhaps the most preeminent voice for AI safety in the party.

His next move is Who Decides: a campaign to make AI safety a standard Democratic position - 'like being pro-choice' - by shaping the environment 2028 presidential hopefuls run in. The vehicle is a closed-door conference at San Francisco's Four Seasons in the brief post-midterm window, gathering presidential hopefuls, labor leaders, super-PAC donors and strategists. Bores's own framing: they can't match the industry tit-for-tat in spending, 'but that's all they have to offer. We will have public opinion and public support, and we will be shaping the terms of the debate.'

The profile catches the structural oddity without resolving it: the party's leading AI-safety champion is himself funded partly by tech money, and his theory of change runs through donor convenings rather than primary voters. What is clear is the stakes both sides see: AI policy is becoming a presidential-primary litmus test two full years before Iowa.
