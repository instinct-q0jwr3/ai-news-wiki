AI in schools – The choice we keep making

type: news-summary · created: 2026-09-20 · updated: 2026-09-22 · confidence: high

hacker-news

Summary

Ivan Johnson, head of Friends School Boulder, argues that education keeps facing the same choice with every new technology, listen to the sellers or listen to teachers, and that AI is the latest round. His historical throughline: 1950s-60s broadcast television pitched as one 'master teacher' reaching hundreds of classrooms, and the 2012 MOOC 'tsunami' predicted by Stanford president John Hennessy, neither of which replaced the classroom because the core of education, a skilled present teacher with a small group of kids, has never been made cheaper without being made worse.

He backs the point with two personal failures: spending over $50,000 on smart boards that ate 75 percent of whiteboard space and worked a quarter of the time until teachers retreated to their whiteboard strips, and opening the gradebook live to students and parents in 2008, a 'transparency' move whose cost was human rather than financial. His Upton Sinclair framing: it is hard to get someone to see clearly when their income depends on not seeing it.

Highlights

  • Thesis: education technology always pitches 'more kids, more consistently, for less money', and it never works for the core teacher-student relationship.
  • Precedent 1: 1950s-60s classroom television, one 'master teacher' broadcast to hundreds of rooms, did not hold up.
  • Precedent 2: Hennessy's 2012 'Coming Tsunami' keynote and MOOCs; completion rates stayed famously terrible and universities were not replaced.
  • Automation paradox: technology makes cars and banking cheaper, but has never made teaching cheaper without making it worse.
  • Smart boards: $50,000+ spent over teachers' objections; they consumed 75% of whiteboard space and worked ~25% of the time.
  • Open gradebooks (2008): live grades framed as transparency; the cost was human, not financial.
  • Cites Upton Sinclair on efficiency valued over humanity: hard to see clearly when income depends on not seeing.
  • Counter-image: a Waldorf teacher's freehand chalk drawing of a cell, deeper than any textbook or slide.
