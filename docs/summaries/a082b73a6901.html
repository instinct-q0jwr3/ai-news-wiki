For nearly two years, one comforting idea has shaped how companies and government contractors adopt open-weight AI models that anyone can download and run.

If we run the weights on our own servers, the thinking goes, our data can't leave the building and our systems are secure.

Highlights

  • It's easy to see why that idea caught on.
  • Security chiefs and policymakers in Ottawa, Washington (DC), London, Berlin, and Tokyo have been offered two kinds of black box.
  • The first is the closed API from a big lab: send proprietary code and sensitive data into someone else's cloud and take the vendor's word that the model is safe, without ever seeing the data lineage or the trade-offs around safety…
  • The second looked like the way out: download an open-weight model, host it on your own hardware, and you don't have to trust the provider at all.
  • Thousands of engineering teams have taken the second road, and a growing share took it with Chinese models, which are capable and cheap.
  • When lawmakers in Washington opened an investigation into corporate use of Chinese models this spring, one chief executive summed up a view common in boardrooms well beyond the United States, "An open-source model does not have access…
  • It doesn't work that way," implying that simply hosting a model in a trusted environment makes it safe.
  • Once a model can type into a command line, whatever was baked into its weights during training gets a keyboard inside your network.
