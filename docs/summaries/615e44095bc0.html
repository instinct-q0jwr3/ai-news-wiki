Mass attempted replication of old findings of LLMs with new models

type: news-summary · created: 2026-11-03 · updated: 2026-11-03 · confidence: high

Summary

How long does a published finding about LLMs stay true? This large-scale replication effort re-ran 50 widely cited LLM findings - from fabricated citations and sycophancy to jailbreaks, long context and cultural bias - across 20 models released between July 2024 and September 2026, from eight developers. The answer: within 15 months of publication, most claims either no longer held or had substantially weakened. By September 2026, 44% of the tested claims had 'expired' (relative effect size down to a fifth or less of the original), another 34% had weakened, 16% persisted, and only 6% had strengthened.

The canonical example: in 2023, 55% of ChatGPT's literature-review citations were fake; the same prompts sent to GPT-6.1-Sol and Claude Sonnet 5.5 now rarely produce fabrications. Persistence varies by topic - five of nine reasoning claims and six of eleven sycophancy-and-deception claims expired, while judges' self-preference and nonuniform random-number generation actually strengthened.

The methodological point is the uncomfortable one for the field: claims made on yesterday's models cannot be assumed to hold on today's, so a large fraction of the empirical foundation people cite about 'how LLMs behave' describes models nobody uses anymore. The authors fit trend lines per claim as a function of model release date, which turns 'LLMs are like X' from a durable finding into a time-stamped measurement. For anyone citing 2024-era behavior papers in 2026 arguments, this is the corrective.

Highlights

  • Mass replication: 50 widely cited LLM claims re-tested across 20 models (July 2024-Sept 2026, 8 developers)
  • 44% of claims expired within ~15 months, 34% weakened, 16% persist, 6% strengthened
  • Example: fabricated citations fell from 55% (GPT-3.5 era) to rare in GPT-6.1-Sol and Claude Sonnet 5.5
  • Implication: most published 'how LLMs behave' findings describe models nobody uses anymore - behavioral claims are time-stamped measurements, not durable facts
