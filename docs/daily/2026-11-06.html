The acceleration question

METR's assessment, with most evidence redacted, puts Anthropic's progress at about 2x.

AI physics startup PSI (Physical Superintelligence) says a research system running a non-public Anthropic model proved the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture, open since 2008, which concerns when quantum computers can be exponentially faster than ordinary ones. The page quotes an outside expert calling it a huge result that draws on analysis of boolean functions and probabilistic combinatorics. PSI frames it against Shor's 1994 factoring algorithm as context. This is the company's own announcement; independent verification of the proof is not shown in the text read. (read more).

A LessWrong shortform post by Arjun Panickssery says he struggles to believe autonomous AI R&D is ingenious. In his side projects the model's judgment is basic-level poor: eight hackathon papers he judged scored 60-95% on Pangram, were similar and methodologically incomplete in the same way. The exception is hill-climbing tasks with fast feedback: he improved the SOTA for listing legal Scrabble moves 10x by prompting Astra repeatedly, though key leaps came from his own suggestions. Astra also writes hard-to-follow reports. A reply agrees that AIs need human direction. A single person's anecdote. (read more).

METR published an independent assessment of how much AI is speeding up AI R&D at Anthropic, based on a high-access evaluation from Aug. 12 to Oct. 29, 2026 with internal metrics on pretraining, RL, compute, benchmarks, speedup analyses, forecasts and staff interviews. Their conclusion: most METR staff now think Anthropic is improving model capabilities on well-specified tasks at about twice the rate of March 2024 to October 2025 (a 1.9x estimate; 1.3x or 3x would not surprise them), with less certainty for fuzzier tasks. Major caveat: Anthropic redacted most of the evidence METR found most informative, citing information that could accelerate other developers. METR says this means the public must rely on its judgment more than ideal. High-level conclusions on risk were shareable. (read more).

Ajeya Cotra argues, from METR's public report on Anthropic, that AI is dramatically accelerating AI research. Anthropic's S-1 says it 'may imminently meet' the Responsible Scaling Policy threshold of progress twice as fast as the fastest sustained pre-AI rate. METR found capabilities progress over Oct 2025 to Oct 2026 about twice as fast as the prior twelve months, measured by an internal Epoch Capabilities Index (AECI), with most of the evidence redacted. She reads this as the threshold already crossed and as the most important AI-risk update in years, pointing to shorter timelines. Her reasoning: if overall capability progress doubles, software 'compute multipliers' must grow roughly 2.5-3x faster, so human researchers are being sped up by much more than 2x. She did not see the unredacted report. (read more).

HotHardware describes OpenAI's Linux kernel experiment: a multi-tier pipeline of scout agents (pick audit targets), analyst agents (flag suspicious logic), triage systems (spin up isolated VMs to prove bugs are real) and exploit agents (test against defenses like KASLR and SMEP, package proofs of concept and draft patches for human review). A GitHub issue on the Claude Code repo reports that the tool put the user's email address into the User-Agent header of web requests while researching, which the reporter says led to dozens of scam emails a day. He says it should be removed from the harness, not be a setting, and says he is moving his company to Pi with OpenRouter. A maintainer reply says nobody built this as a feature: models are unpredictable. The scenario is a site like Wikipedia that requires scripted clients to identify themselves with a contact email, so Claude includes the user's email in its curl call. Versions 2.1.317 and later add a default-on built-in mod, cc-plugin-privacy, that deterministically blocks it (switch off via /plugins), and its source is available for review. Maintainer claims are unverified by this summary. (read more).

A write-up from pwn.ai shows an escape from Firecracker, the microVM meant to isolate untrusted code, achieving root code execution on the host. Building on a host-memory primitive from Part 1, the authors find Firecracker in memory and take over a live vCPU thread: while the thread sits in the KVM_RUN ioctl, its saved registers (pt_regs) control where it resumes, so altering them makes it run a chosen call on return. Firecracker keeps running and the VM stays reachable over SSH. It is tied to a CVE identifier in the post. (read more).

Cloudflare argues that AI agents acting on the web strip sites of the ability to recognize, serve and hold accountable the users behind them, so sites bear the cost when something goes wrong. The research shelf

How scientists use Claude, NVIDIA's world-simulation models, and training handbooks.

Anthropic's research report studies how scientists use Claude, Claude Code and Claude Science with privacy-preserving infrastructure plus a survey. Usage varies by field: formal sciences like computer science, math and statistics use Claude more per researcher, while health and lab-based physical sciences use it less. By task, scientists use it more for analyzing data and writing, less for gathering data or generating ideas and hypotheses. Use is higher where a 'theoretical AI capability' measure is higher, in tasks that are more cognitive and suit reinforcement learning. The authors conclude scientists use AI where it works best, and outline scenarios for how AI could accelerate research. Anthropic studying its own product. (read more).

The data commons

AI scrapers are overwhelming university libraries.

CBC reports that university libraries worldwide are being swarmed by AI data-scraping bots that slow or crash sites meant for students, researchers and the public, sometimes knocking whole collections offline. Reports began in late 2024 across North America, Europe and Oceania and keep getting worse. San Jose State's Jill Strykowski says the bots are 'bombarding us', and Victoria's Dean Seeman says traffic sometimes takes sites down completely. Libraries and museums are prime targets because their vetted content sits mostly outside paywalls and logins. Ontario Tech's Peter Lewis says ordinary users now compete with bot farms, an extractive use of public-interest institutions to build commercial models. (read more).
