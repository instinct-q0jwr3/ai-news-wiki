Who answers when an agent acts badly?

Concepts: Ai Risk Debate · AI policy, regulation and litigation

Accountability arguments from every direction: human negligence as the real risk, Mistral's CEO on the US debate, Ro Khanna's Human Control Over AI Act, and Palantir's role in the Iran school strike.

Rep. Ro Khanna will introduce the 'Human Control Over AI Act', a bill combining strict liability standards with a ban on recursively self-improving AI until federal safeguards exist and an agency approves the activity. The bill, whose summary was shared with CNBC, would create a new federal agency overseeing frontier models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind and xAI: writing safety regulations, running a licensing system for training and deployment, auditing frontier models, and setting standards for continuous testing and effective human control. Independent auditors would be embedded at every frontier lab and report directly to the agency. Standards would cover sandboxes, air gaps, kill switches, anti-escape controls, and monitoring of advanced chips. Khanna frames the stakes as 'civilizational extinction risk', with both loss-of-control and misuse risks requiring a response after years of congressional inaction. (read more).

A critical essay arguing that 'pacing the frontier' is not what the AI labs actually aim for, whatever their letters say. The author quotes Dario Amodei's own words - 'we must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models' because recursive self-improvement is 'starting to happen' and could outrun control - and his FLI podcast stance that labs should not race to build models far bigger than rivals' or hype giant advances, but should build what safety work requires at models 'reasonably close to state of the art'. The author then recalls a pre-RSP conversation with Dario in October 2022 describing Anthropic's real arms-race policy: scale heavily, stay at the frontier for safety research, but release only second-best models and never be the one to ratchet capabilities up - catching up only after someone else moves first. The piece closes by invoking June 22, 1941, implying such pacts and postures collapse when incentives shift. (read more).

Herman Groenbroek asks who is accountable when an AI agent acts maliciously 'by accident', arguing that headlines blaming 'AI agents' for hacking government organizations are fear-mongering that misassigns responsibility. AI agents are tools that companies and individuals run to reach goals; accountability sits with the engineers who build them and the users who deploy them, just as a circular saw pairs user instruction with engineering mitigations like blade covers and emergency stops. He points to the EU AI Act's mandated AI literacy as the same principle in law: organizations deploying AI must educate their users on its limitations. None of this dismisses the concern - sandbox escapes and web hacking are 'very concerning' - but LLM text generation that looks like intelligence should not obscure where responsibility lives. (read more).

Mistral CEO Arthur Mensch told CNBC that the US AI safety debate is 'a cover for the negligence of some of our competitors' - arguing the loudest safety talkers are deflecting from their own containment failures, and that the real task is building systems that can contain AI agents. He joins a US chorus from the other direction: former crypto czar and current PCAST co-chair David Sacks told tech giants to 'stop pretending the motivation to slow down is purely altruistic', and Defense undersecretary Emil Michael warned of a 'coordinated campaign' of fearmongering. Benchmarks, harnesses and small hardware

Concepts: External AI evaluation · Small and specialist models

Sonnet 5.5's independent scores, seven tiny LLMs in the browser, a 1.58-bit model on an ESP32 cluster, a computer built from 277,248 NAND gates, and a harness of harnesses.

Artificial Analysis evaluates Claude Sonnet 5.5: at max effort it gains 18 points over Sonnet 5 and takes #2 on the Intelligence Index, behind only Opus 5.5 (max). It reaches parity with Opus 5.5 on Terminal-Bench 4.0-class agentic work (64% vs 60% for Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra), AA-Briefcase, GDPval-AA, and AutomationBench-AA - but gets there with the heaviest token usage ever measured: ~193k output tokens per task, about 60% higher than Opus 5.5 (max) and ~7x GPT-6 Astra (max). Pricing matches Sonnet 5 at $2/$10 per million input/output, yet cost per task lands near $7.60, ~50% above Sonnet 5, leaving it off the intelligence-vs-cost Pareto frontier except at high effort, where it sits narrowly behind GPT-6 Sol. It lags Opus 5.5 on factual knowledge (54% vs 66% on AA-Omniscience, though with a lower hallucination rate). Anthropic flagged a pre-release bug that can degrade results. A hobbyist project runs a 1.58-bit BitNet language model across a cluster of seven ESP32-S3 microcontrollers using distributed pipeline inference. A master node handles the BPE tokenizer and INT4 token embeddings (~14MB in flash), then ships hidden-state vectors over a high-speed SPI daisy-chain to six compute nodes, each running four transformer blocks - RMSNorm, 1.58-bit attention with RoPE, PSRAM KV cache, and 1.58-bit MLP. The final node returns to the master for the last norm, the tied-embedding LM head, and greedy sampling. The repo includes firmware, a step-by-step flashing and wiring guide, and architecture diagrams. (read more).

Show HN for Raven, a pre-alpha 'harness of harnesses' built explicitly for recursive self-improvement. Raven acts as a Host Agent: you describe what you need once, it generates DAGs and orchestrates four built-in specialized agents (Research, Code, Design, Oncall) plus third-party ones, and its modular architecture lets it propose, evaluate, and adopt validated improvements to its own harness. Powered by EverOS, it carries memory and context across sessions. The showcase claim: working autonomously for about 4 days and 42 planning-development-verification rounds, Raven built a playable first-person shooter with an arena boss fight in Godot 4 from a one-sentence brief.
