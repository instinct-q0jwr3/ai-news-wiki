The discipline holds

An engineer's case for keeping decisions reviewable while agents implement, and Postgres 19 slipping because LLMs find bugs faster than committers can fix them.

PostgreSQL's release train has been clockwork for a decade - mid-September to mid-October - and this year it will miss: version 19 most likely slips to November, possibly further, and three large features (UPDATE/DELETE FOR PORTION OF, SQL Property Graph Queries, ALTER TABLE ... MERGE/SPLIT PARTITION) were reverted unusually late in the beta. Robert Haas's short answer to what happened: LLMs happened. Postgres has always had plenty of bugs in thirty-odd years of code; what changed is that finding them became vastly easier, while fixing them got only somewhat easier. The numbers make the case. Between feature freeze and late September, back-patched commits to the in-development stable branch jumped more than 60 percent year over year (724 to 1169) while master-branch commits slightly declined - and back-ports to the prior release jumped over 74 percent (248 to 433). Nobody on the project believes committers suddenly got sloppier; a huge number of people on the internet are now reporting LLM-found bugs, and the committers have been very, very busy fixing them. That flood explains both the late reverts and the delay: LLMs can draft fixes, but the fixes are not always correct or compliant with project conventions, so each report still costs senior reviewer time. The bug backlog of a 30-year-old codebase is being drained at machine speed, and the bottleneck is the humans who merge. (read more).

An engineer's answer to 'AI has transformed software development' is to ask which part, exactly, got transformed. Producing an implementation, exploring a design, and deciding whether a system solves the right problem are connected but distinct - and automating one settles none of the others. The piece's claim: agents can absorb more implementation work and assist with analysis, design and testing, while engineers remain responsible for deciding what to build and what evidence is sufficient to accept it. The author resists the 'replacing the typist' framing - it understates agents and turns task automation into a jobs claim that requires evidence beyond a good demo. The practical core is about memory and accountability. A chat is convenient for exploring options and a fragile place to keep the only record of a decision: context is bounded, sessions get summarized and compacted, and a summary can preserve the conclusion while losing the condition that made it reasonable ('use a local cache' is not 'use a local cache because this screen must work offline'). The recommendation: give durable decisions a small, reviewable home - a PROJECT_GUIDE.md wired into the tool's project instructions - supply each increment with the relevant files, and ask the agent to paraphrase intent, constraints and acceptance cases before implementing. Markdown guarantees neither retrieval nor obedience, and any claim of savings needs measurement of the complete task, not the prompt. (read more).

Israeli startup 42AI has come out of stealth with a $62.5 million seed round and a claim that sounds like science fiction: a 'behavioral world model' that predicts what individual people will do before they decide to do it. Its first market is financial brokerages, where the company says the model predicts how individual traders respond to volatile conditions - ten large brokerages are live, using it for liquidity, margin requirements and hedging exposure. The method is psychometrics at scale. Rather than extrapolating from past actions, 42AI watches how people react to ostensibly unrelated events - KYC flows, onboarding, support calls, money transfers - measuring sentiment, voice, and interaction counts, then converts those observations into mathematical trait representations fed into a transformer-based world model. 'In a world model, every individual is the world,' CTO Ehud Ezra says. Training rewards penalize the model when a simulation breaks a person's underlying tendencies - a risk-averse user suddenly modeled as a risk-taker means 'the target is off.' A separate LLM generates explanations of the predictions. The claims deserve the caveats attached. The headline '98% accuracy' turns out to mean trading flows falling within a predicted range, not individual trades called correctly; the F1 score for direction and volume is 0.8-0.85. CEO Amit Gal-Or says the model 'never knows who the person is,' yet the company builds individual behavioral profiles from linked customer activity across datasets covering almost 30 million people. The team spent three years on the approach with advisors including psychology and PTSD experts and Nobel chemistry laureate Dan Shechtman, claims one brokerage gained ~$30 million via dynamic hedging, and reports seven-figure revenue. Whether behavioral world models generalize beyond brokerage floors is now a $62.5 million question. (read more).

Measuring the frontier

SemiAnalysis launches GDPx: eleven high-ROI domains, safety classifiers and all - and the best model on Earth scores 12%.

SemiAnalysis has built GDPx on a deliberately unfashionable premise: the first trillion dollars of AI revenue will concentrate in sectors that can deploy frontier intelligence at high ROI, so a benchmark weighted by GDP share or occupational taxonomies measures the wrong thing. Their alternative spans eleven chosen domains - chip design, cybersecurity, biology, finance, legal, quant trading, recursive self-improvement, hardware design, materials science, alignment and software engineering - while deliberately skipping government, real estate and insurance. It is brutal by design: the best model in the world scores 12 percent. The rankings come with caveats the authors consider features. Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra lose points to their own safety classifiers; excluding cyber, bio and alignment reshuffles the board. Overall, Opus 5.5 is the best all-rounder (winning quant trading, hardware and chip design), Gemini 4 Carbon is Google's real step up - best in bio, edging software engineering, the only model in Opus/Astra's ballpark - and Astra wins RSI and materials science convincingly. Finance and legal tasks are so hard that essentially every model scores zero. On the cost-performance Pareto, Luna and DeepSeek fight the low end, OpenAI holds the middle, Opus takes the high end. One underrated practical note: safety classifiers should drive model choice per use case - Muse Wonder Full, they say, is meaningfully the best cyber model available. (read more).

The bill for the troves

Doctorow's 'Autodoxing': twenty-five years of surveillance policy was foreseen, and AI-era identification is collecting on it.

Cory Doctorow's 'Autodoxing' is a history lesson with a bill attached. For twenty-five years, he writes, the wreckage of bad tech policy was both foreseeable and foreseen: when Scott McNealy declared privacy dead, digital rights advocates answered that a society-wide abolition of privacy sets up infinite harms. Clinton tried to backdoor every microchip; the NSA banned civilian access to working encryption; when that fell, governments encouraged the private sector to spy without limit, and anticircumvention law made it illegal to reverse-engineer our own devices to bolt privacy back on. The resulting private troves of intimate data became a warrantless, off-the-books surveillance system that cops and spies tap at will - and the firms holding them cannot keep them safe. Exhibit A is Equifax, which doxed 148 million Americans nearly a decade ago and has since been allowed to absorb more commercial surveillance firms on three continents. The column's turn is what happens when that machinery meets AI-era identification: the same harvested data that powers ad-tech and government lookups now lets anyone be unmasked at scale - autodoxing - and the policy response arriving first (age verification, ID mandates) builds yet more identity databases on top of the ones that keep leaking. Doctorow's argument runs the other way: the road out is not more collection but less - the data that is never gathered cannot be breached, subpoenaed or repurposed. After a quarter century of being right too early, the essay reads as a demand that the next pile of identity data not be built at all. (read more).

An architect tested 13 AI models on the task his profession actually runs on - construction documents - and found the line was crossed this fall. Stephen Gallagher gave each model the same starter pack: rough concept sketches for a simple house plus a reference drawing set he had drafted years ago in AutoCAD. The results ran from Claude Opus 3's outright refusal (2024) through cartoonish sketches (2025 and early 2026 models) to Claude Opus 5.5, released late September 2026, producing a set Gallagher judges good enough to submit for a building permit. Two caveats matter. The models drew directly, without CAD software, which means no parametric checking behind the geometry. And the test wasn't sterile: Gallagher redlined the models' work the way a senior architect corrects junior staff, so the best results include human-in-the-loop feedback - which he argues is the realistic condition, since that is how architectural practice actually works. The weakest models got no feedback because they weren't worth correcting. His conclusion is aimed at his own profession, which he says is mostly unaware of the shift because it arrived suddenly and because extracting permit-quality output takes careful prompting. The comparison he reaches for is software engineering over the past year: direct manual production replaced by supervision of increasingly capable models. 'Wile E. Agents do the work

An agent drives stereotaxic arms for mouse brain surgery under a vet's kill switch, Better Together lets personal agents gang up on spam, and inside the frontier labs the agent workdays already outnumber the human ones.

A researcher with friends at the frontier labs describes what recursive self-improvement looks like from the inside, and it is mundane in the way revolutions often are: researchers start the day asking their agent what they should do; the tests they used to run are the agent's job; the human steps in where the agent gets stuck - approving a PR, unblocking another team. His test for an 'AGI organization': does what it builds go straight back into building the next version of itself? An insurer's claims AI never will; the frontier labs' tooling already does. The numbers he cites: at OpenAI's research org, every human workday was matched by 3.1 agent workdays as of mid-August, after agent runtime passed human working time in June; at Anthropic, more than 80 percent of merged code by line count was written by Claude as of May. Researcher Zuxin Liu wrote in September that he had stopped writing code by hand two months earlier - his workspace is Codex and Slack. The argument: internet-era companies were never built this way, and the frontier labs have rebuilt their organization, context and infrastructure around how AI works - which is why the next kind of company is taking shape there first. The party tricks are real results. Junior-engineer hiring has collapsed - Stanford's 'Canaries in the Coal Mine' work puts employment for developers aged 22-25 nearly 20% below its late-2022 peak while senior hiring keeps growing, and Anthropic says juniors have fallen from 27% to 9% of its engineering hires since 2025. Laurie Voss's new post argues the industry's real problem is that it deleted the job juniors were trained for and never built a way to interview for the new one. The old filter is dead: coding interviews measured programming, and programming is now the part agents do. Companies are improvising - one Anthropic team dropped coding interviews entirely, Canva made an AI-assisted round mandatory, Meta swapped one onsite round, Google is piloting code-comprehension rounds - but nobody claims a working answer, so many firms simply only hire seniors, which Voss calls totally unsustainable. The altruistic argument (train juniors or the industry dies) fails under capitalism because every manager prefers that some other company pay for the training. Supply-chain security

Hacktron's AI pentest agent found a backtick email address that led to root on GNU Savannah and a confirmed path to push commits to upstream Bash as its maintainer - the xz scenario, found in days against volunteer-run infrastructure. The FSF fixed all 26 reported vulnerabilities.

Security firm Hacktron pointed its AI pentest agent at GNU Savannah - the volunteer-run forge that hosts Bash, Coreutils and most of the GNU userland - and walked away with a path to backdooring Bash itself. The root bug is almost embarrassing: Savane's email validator accepts addresses like atouch${IFS}/tmp/savane-rce-proof@attacker.example, and its 'Test GPG keys' feature concatenates the account email straight into a shell command. Any registered user got code execution on the backend. From there the chain was methodical: read the database credentials from Savane's config, flip the superuser flag on their own account, use the admin 'Become this user' box to impersonate Chet Ramey - Bash's maintainer - and confirm the site would accept a new SSH key granting push access to bash.git. They stopped before adding one. An attacker who didn't stop could have pushed commits to upstream Bash, Coreutils, or any Savannah project, packaged downstream to millions of machines - the xz scenario, minus the years of patient social engineering. An unpatched 5.15 kernel with the 2023 GameOver(lay) OverlayFS bugs gave them root and access to a GNU backup server for good measure. The window was about two months: the FSF traced the vulnerable code path to the March 7, 2026 Savane release, and Hacktron reported on May 11. The full sweep found 26 confirmed vulnerabilities, all fixed, with an incident report published. Two lessons matter beyond GNU: AI-assisted auditing is now good enough to find internet-critical bugs in a weekend against infrastructure maintained by a handful of volunteers - and the security of the entire free-software supply chain still rests on exactly that infrastructure. The backlash

Ketan Joshi dissects a Microsoft Copilot ad in which 'Kyle' copies a praised colleague's work and takes the credit - and calls it the most honest piece of AI marketing ever made: the product truth is appropriation, sold with a smile.

Ketan Joshi has found the 30 seconds that, for him, explain generative AI better than any whitepaper: a Microsoft Copilot ad in which an office worker named Kyle watches a colleague, Daniel, get praised in the team channel for a forecast model called 'honestly, beautiful' - then turns to Copilot with 'analyse Daniel's model,' 'improve my charts,' 'make mine beautiful,' and collects the applause himself. Joshi's shot-by-shot reading is gleeful and vicious: the first seconds, he notes, are staged as a horror movie, down to the coworker's praise reflected in Kyle's eyeball. His argument is that the ad is an unusually honest piece of product marketing. Most AI advertising is vague aspiration; this one dramatizes a specific use case - appropriating a colleague's work and passing it off as your own, faster. 'People who have been stealing from people they consider beneath them their whole lives,' he writes, 'feel an intense sense of understanding with a software system designed from its absolute core to be a theft machine.' He connects it to his own experience of having a blog post ripped and reworded by an outlet that added an AI-generated header image. The piece lands because the ad's own details do the work: the narrator's 'he wouldn't say he's competitive... out loud,' the whispered 'my charts,' the precise 9:42am timestamp 'like he's recounting the exact moment of a terrorist bombing inside Kyle's soul.' Whether or not one accepts Joshi's politics, the ad is a strange artifact - a trillion-dollar company selling its flagship AI feature as a remedy for the unbearable sight of a colleague being praised.
