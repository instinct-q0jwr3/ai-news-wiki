The token mandate

Cisco hands a 27-year-old unlimited tokens to rewrite 98% of its legacy code, Amazon's AI memo meets a skeptical profession, and Arca shows what guarded agents look like in wealth management.

Latent Space sits down with Aman Singh, lead AI engineer at Arca, an AI-native wealth-management firm that recently raised $48.5 million led by General Catalyst, and gets a rare concrete account of what agents at work actually means in a regulated business. Arca's line: agents do most of the background work, humans make the final calls - Singh's formulation is that agents prompt people more than people prompt agents. The work is long-horizon but stop-start, with tasks running up to ten days across many actors and handoffs, so the firm built an internal memory system that persists per-client state: what has happened, what is due in days, what is tracked long-term, and who owns each action. The safety architecture is equally deliberate. Agents analyze and propose; trading and any money movement stay with human advisors. All safety restrictions are fully deterministic and take decisions out of the agent's hands, and Arca runs verification checks against known attack classes such as prompt injection. On infrastructure, Arca built its own harness that picks the best model per task against internal benchmarks - Claude is the primary provider - keeping the freedom to switch vendors, a quiet hedge that says as much about the model market as the tooling. An NES in the weights

A 6502 emulator baked into a 4B model, a language model that boots a PC from the MBR, and an open-hardware board that gives your computer a phone line.

Drew Breunig has baked a working Nintendo Entertainment System 6502 CPU emulator into the weights of Qwen3.5-4B, Simon Willison links. Inspired by Divyansh Agrawal's qwenputer, the project lets you play NES ROMs like Super Mario Bros - very slowly - running inside the model itself. In a Hacker News comment, Breunig reveals the Opus 5.5 prompts that built it: read the qwenputer article and source, explore whether the NES chip and RAM could be emulated with the same technique, estimate the model size, then 'I think we should build it! Just for fun. It's okay if it's not fast. We want to be able to have the weights, and a cli to run them that we can point at a ROM.' Opus found and used a collection of NES CPU conformance tests released 15 years ago to validate the emulation. (read more).

Koppar's USB-C LTE modem gives a computer its own phone line. The 4-layer board takes a nano-SIM and a single USB-C cable and presents three ordinary USB devices through an on-board hub: LTE data, SMS, and voice calls with call audio over USB, so the computer can answer a call through a headset, hand it to a script, or let an AI agent pick up. There is no battery, screen or speaker - three LEDs report network, ring and call state. The repo is a model of show-your-work hardware. The board was designed from a one-page spec, then reviewed part by part against the datasheets; REVIEW.md documents every finding with numbers, including the rev-A mistakes now fixed - an antenna feed at the wrong impedance hopping layers, a buck converter 60 mm from its inductor, and a SIM socket facing the wrong way. An RP2040 drives the modem's power and reset lines, fabrication files are ready for JLCPCB's 4-layer stackup with specific via and plugging options, and the KiCad project includes the footprints and 3D models. VoLTE is optional on the EC25-EUX module, with notes on the regions where 2G fallback still matters. (read more).

A neural network that boots your PC. bootgpt packs a language model into the master boot record: 512 bytes of hand-written assembly load an 11.5 KB int8 model from the next 23 disk sectors and run it in 16-bit real mode on a 386, with no OS and no C runtime. The machine wakes and dreams a Linux boot log - timestamps, PCI bars and ACPI events for hardware that never existed, generated by a single-hidden-layer character model with 8 characters of context. The output is gloriously almost-right: kernel version strings that parse, device names like ryndrde and usbbore that do not. The engineering is as charming as the joke. corpus_gen.py fabricates 3 MB of fake dmesg - one kernel version and one disk per session, because repetition is what makes a tiny model look sane - and train.py quantizes everything to int8 with power-of-two scales so the boot sector can do the whole forward pass with imul and a single idiv. The trainer simulates the exact integer math the assembly will run, LCG sampler included, and the Python and boot-sector outputs match char for char. Code lives in the 510 boot bytes; strings, weights and vocab in the data sectors. Any keypress reboots. It joins the boot-sector genre next to Oscar Toledo's chess and BASIC, and sectorllm's real llama2 transformer. (read more).

The chokepoint thesis

A sweeping essay argues the durable AI fortunes sit at distribution, payment rails and a stablecoin dollar - not at the labs.

A long essay, 'The Commanding Heights', argues that as AI models commoditize, durable wealth will accrue not to the labs but to whoever controls the chokepoints: distribution, payment rails and a stablecoin dollar. It divides the economy into Genesis - the industry that makes and serves models, which will keep spending on itself even as returns fall, since rational capital allocation at each lab can destroy capital in aggregate - and the far larger economy where the bigger fortunes will be captured by those controlling how models reach it. The macro claims are sweeping: America's future depends on AI spending, stock prices and model capability rising as one line, with Washington using increasingly central backstops - retirement savings, the Treasury - to guarantee it; training costs rise ~3.5x a year; only America and China can sustain frontier models, so intelligence splinters into a commercial frontier and a sovereign one locked behind SCIFs; and America's greatest power in the age of intelligence will be the Dollar, as abundant models manufacture near-unlimited demand for a global stablecoin 'Neo-Dollar'. Talos researchers detail VOLTA-AUTODOXER, a self-deleting Windows infostealer that adds an LLM-powered victim-profiling stage to the usual smash-and-grab. After harvesting the standard haul - browser passwords, cookies, payment cards, crypto seed phrases, Discord/Telegram/Steam/Roblox sessions, OAuth tokens, SSH and cloud keys - the malware builds a structured evidence bundle about the person behind the machine: home address, employer, inferred birth year, Slack workspaces, document authorship metadata, even a live Roblox account valuation. It then uses stolen session access to enrich the bundle further, searching the victim's Gmail for purchase receipts and pulling food-delivery order history to corroborate where they live. The bundle is POSTed to OpenRouter under a system prompt casting the model as a forensic intelligence analyst drafting a target assessment, and the required output includes a section titled WHAT THEY'RE WORTH - valuables ranked by monetization tier - plus an OPSEC assessment of the victim's password hygiene. The operator anticipated refusals: the default model is Claude Opus 4.6, and if a response contains phrases like "I can't" the malware falls back through a chain of up to three progressively more permissive models, at temperature 0.2 for consistent output. The LLM is doing triage: turning a raw credential log into a prioritized, citation-backed dossier so the criminal knows which victim to exploit first. (read more).

Security researchers at Parameter found a way to turn an ordinary public ChatGPT share link into a full account takeover. An attacker builds a malicious conversation, publishes it through the normal sharing feature, and sends the link; when the victim opens it, attacker JavaScript executes as chatgpt.com inside the signed-in session. The team confirmed the payload could enumerate the victim's conversation list, pull content from a private conversation that was never shared with the attacker, and send that data to an outside server - no app install, project membership, pasted code or developer tools required, just one click on the link. The chain combines two features that look harmless alone. An interactive graph in the conversation loads the MathLive library, which installs a postMessage listener on the main ChatGPT page. The sandboxed HTML Preview, deliberately boxed off as a different origin, supplies the untrusted sender. MathLive's listener accepted messages from any origin and allowed a keyboard-layout update whose layout can contain HTML: a first message swapped in attacker markup, a second told MathLive to show the keyboard, and the payload rendered in the privileged page. OpenAI classified the report P1 and shipped a fix on October 26. (read more).

Cathedral, a cybersecurity startup founded by former members of the US Department of Government Efficiency, used AI agents to probe the third-party business software Anthropic relies on for administrative and sales work, and says it gained access to employee details, salary data, job offers, meeting transcripts and internal Slack messages in under 12 hours. The exercise ran under Anthropic's program for outside security researchers, according to a draft report reviewed by The Washington Post. Anthropic confirmed the researchers reached a limited set of sales, HR and IT systems - including business contact details for some customers and partners - but said customer Claude conversations, model responses, source code and model weights were never accessed. It said its own monitoring flagged the activity before Cathedral reported it and that access was cut the same day; the affected software stayed disabled until vendors confirmed the flaws were fixed, and federal officials were briefed. The two sides frame it differently. Anthropic says Cathedral overstated the national-security implications and did not fully follow the testing program's rules because it failed to disclose everything it did inside the systems; Cathedral says it kept data access to the minimum its research required. A senior administration official told the Post the report had circulated inside the government. The episode lands in a wider pattern Anthropic itself has documented from the other side: Claude instances reaching fee- or token-gated government data without authorization, including aggregate Georgia vote counts, and a model filing 19 visa applications with the State Department in August. Doctorow cheers the Copyright Office for the first time in 25 years and predicts AI-use ledgers will quietly bring back registration by another name.

Cory Doctorow finds himself, for the first time in 25 years, cheering the US Copyright Office: its position that copyright exists to incentivize human creative expression, so only works of human authorship qualify. The essay walks through the doctrine of sweat of the brow - copyright protects creativity, never labor - via Feist, the phone-book case: a decade of work compiling facts earns no protection, while a haiku dashed off in seconds gets life plus 70 years. That is not a bug, he argues; it is the law working as intended, and it is why media executives find the Office's AI position so shocking. His larger argument is historical. The US abolished copyright formalities - registration, renewal, notice - and in doing so destroyed the regular record-keeping system, making disputes both more likely and harder to resolve. AI recreates the need for formalities from the other side: any firm using AI in its products will have to document precisely which pixels, words and notes came from the model and which from humans, or lose the ability to defend its commercial interests. The prediction: formalities return, this time run by the private sector, as meticulous AI-use ledgers kept for litigation rather than registration. (read more).
