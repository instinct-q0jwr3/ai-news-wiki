The proof problem

Anthropic open-sources FlashProve to let users verify which model answered them.

Anthropic released FlashProve, an open-source prototype for provable inference: a way for a user to verify that a response came from the model a provider claims, without access to the weights and without being able to rerun the model. Authors come from Anthropic, EPFL, StarkWare, MIT and Succinct. The motivation is trust. Today a user cannot tell whether a router secretly substituted a cheaper model, or whether an attacker tampered with the token stream. Independent evaluators need to know the model they test is the model that gets served, and customers of third-party routers want to know tokens came from the model they paid for. Verification is hard because frontier weights are private and re-running the model costs too much. The approach relies on cryptographic proofs, such as zero-knowledge proofs, where the provider commits to a model and proves each response came from it. Anthropic frames it as a prototype it wants others to take and improve, and says it could matter for pacing frontier AI and for regulation, since providers could demonstrate they meet commitments. Cost and speed figures were beyond the portion read. (read more).

The cost curve

Revolut moves to self-hosted GLM and cuts coding inference cost about 90%, while Traversal raises $120M for SRE agents.

Revolut says it cut AI coding inference costs by roughly 90% in under a year by making self-hosted models its default instead of relying mainly on commercial services like Claude. David Abitbol, its ops and infrastructure lead, told the AI Engineer conference in New York that the rollout to about 2,000 engineers produced a 60% improvement in coding time and 70% in lead time. The path: Revolut used Gemini in early 2025 and Claude Code in early 2026, but engineers were spending $1,000 to $10,000 a month with unclear return, so it set strict budgets and then looked at self-hosting. It moved from GLM-5.1 to GLM-5.3, now served on 56 Nvidia B200 GPUs. At about $0.30 per million tokens, self-hosted GLM 5.1 cost about a tenth of Codex or Claude Code. The surprising lesson was behavioral: with cheap tokens, engineers stopped rationing and used models for debugging and Jira and Confluence tickets, which Abitbol says produced bigger gains than faster code. GLM 5.1 is large - about 754 billion parameters, 40 billion active, roughly 380 GB of GPU memory for weights. (read more).

Vals AI tested how much reasoning effort changes score and cost, running the top model from seven labs at lowest, middle and leaderboard (usually highest) settings across the eight benchmarks of its Vals Index. At the lowest setting four of seven models kept at least 80% of their score for half the cost or less: Claude Opus 5.5 kept 80% at 16% of the cost, Grok 4.7 kept 86% at 16%, and Gemini 4 Argon kept 99% at 84%. Gemini 4 Argon's middle setting even scored slightly above leaderboard at 78% of the cost. But cheaper is not free. On the whole index, 0 of 7 models matched leaderboard score at the lowest setting. In 22 of 56 model-and-benchmark pairs a cheaper setting matched the leaderboard score, none significantly different, while 33 were significantly lower. With hindsight selection, models gained a median 6.0 points over their leaderboard setting at 64% of the cost, but a real system must choose before seeing results, so real gains would be smaller. The practical lesson is per-task routing of effort, since some tasks need more reasoning than others. (read more).

The data commons

Two essays on what open models need: a posttraining task commons that does not exist, and torrents as the natural way to ship 510 GB of weights.

The essay argues that the AI industry got lucky with pretraining data - Wikipedia, open-source code, Stack Exchange, arXiv, Project Gutenberg and billions of web pages formed a commons that was 'lying around' - and that posttraining has no equivalent. Open models like Nemotron and the author's own still depend on that commons for most pretraining data, and over half of crawler traffic is now for AI, per Cloudflare. The key distinction is that pretraining consumes documents while posttraining consumes tasks. A human task is situated: someone wants something, acts, the world responds, goals shift, and the moment passes. An RL task freezes that into a repeatable object, much as writing freezes an oral story. To preserve one you need at least a world, an intent and, in the author's framing, a way to judge outcomes. Nobody has built a shared public stock of such tasks, so frontier labs build them privately and open efforts lack them. The implication is that truly open post-trained agents depend on a task commons that does not exist yet. Only the first half of the essay was read. (read more).

The post argues that every AI model will end up distributed as a torrent. Its anchor example: DeepSeek released DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 on 17 October 2026 as 48 safetensors shards plus a config and tokenizer, 510 GB in total, in exactly one version. Anyone running an agent on that model needs those exact bytes, not a close copy. That is what suits BitTorrent: files that are large, public, immutable and wanted by many people in identical form. Movie torrents fragment into remuxes, encodes and rips, each with few peers; a model has one canonical set, so all demand concentrates on one swarm. The author compares this to the web of the 1990s, when directories and keyword engines gave way to link-ranked search, suggesting model distribution is at the directory stage. Only the opening was captured, so the proposed solution is not covered. (read more).

The reskilling case

IKEA turned 8,500 call agents into design consultants after Billie took the routine half of its inquiries.

IKEA's Ingka Group deployed an AI assistant called Billie across customer service in 2021. By 2023 it was handling about 47% of inbound inquiries, roughly 3.2 million conversations on product information and recommendations, with operating savings of nearly 13 million euros (about $15 million). The interesting step is what came next. Looking at the other 53%, IKEA saw callers who wanted help designing their homes, which needs taste and contextual judgment. Rather than trying to automate them, Ingka treated them as a demand signal and retrained about 8,500 call-center employees as remote interior design consultants, with training in digital retail sales, room planning and relationship management. Billie takes the operational load while the reskilled staff take the design work. It is held up as a case where automation creates a higher-value human role instead of removing the people. The handmade turn

A manifesto for human-made writing and software against AI-generated everything.

Cory Doctorow starts from a taxonomy of 'AI is being shoved down our throats': bosses demanding reckless AI use, financial engineers pushing it into retirement savings, and the most visible form, user-interface coercion. That last one predates AI. His example is Google Plus, when executives tied product-team bonuses to G+ usage, and Goodhart's Law did the rest: the easiest way to get clicks is to replace the button users press most with a G+ version, which frustrates people instead of winning fans. He then ties it to machine learning. When an AI system finds perverse shortcuts to raise a single number, we call it reward hacking, and programmers keep adding specificity to prevent it (a speedrun without teleporting). Tech bosses' own KPIs behave like objective functions, so the corporate version produces the same outcome. The title applies this to a receipts app, Genius Scan, where the 'fatfinger' is the AI feature occupying the place of the control the user wants. Only the opening was read here, so the receipts-app details are not covered. (read more).

The ballot box vs. the data center

The midterms turn data centers into a campaign issue: Democrats win Texas, Georgia, Ohio and Pennsylvania, Ohio towns vote bans, and AI stocks drop.

Ohio voters rejected AI infrastructure almost everywhere it was on the ballot, with at least 14 municipalities passing bans or restrictions on data centers across 21 ballot measures - more than half of the 41 data-center measures nationwide. The state already has 125 local moratoriums and more than 200 operational data centers, and companies plan up to $40 billion in AI infrastructure there by 2030. The results ran the same way across the state. Sunbury passed a charter amendment banning data centers of 25 MW or more, aimed at a possible Amazon project. Wilmington rejected four rezonings by about 80%. Ashville voted to block a campus already under construction. Perry approved financial-guarantee and noise requirements for tax-break seekers. Most measures came from 13 municipalities banning or heavily restricting 'high impact' sites. Polling matches: Gallup found about 70% of Americans oppose local data centers, and an Ohio poll found 55% opposed versus 22% in support, with 65% opposed to tax breaks. Oracle has scrapped a roughly $7 billion, five-year deal to lease about 100,000 advanced AI chips to Tencent after yielding to pressure from the Trump administration, per Financial Times sources. It reflects tension for US AI infrastructure groups doing business with Chinese tech giants. (read more 🔒).

Alibaba, Tencent, ByteDance and other Chinese AI companies ordered 6 million Nvidia RTX Pro 5500 chips worth $70 billion but expect to receive only about 1% within four months, per Bloomberg sources. Developers expect only a small fraction of the ordered chips over the next several months. (read more 🔒).

The Financial Times reports that experts warn slow US grid expansion could stifle the AI boom and give China an edge, as China builds power infrastructure faster. The US is building electricity lines at a quarter of the rate of a decade ago. (read more 🔒).

Geopolitechs, citing a STAR Market Daily report from industry sources, says DeepSeek plans to pre-train its next-generation model, DeepSeek V5, on Huawei Ascend chips. That would be a shift, since Ascend has mostly served inference rather than pre-training for DeepSeek. The ties go back to early 2025, when Huawei reportedly deployed more than 3,000 Ascend chips to support DeepSeek V3 and R1 on Huawei Cloud. After DeepSeek V4 in April 2026, Huawei said Ascend SuperPoD systems could fully support the V4 family, and on September 30 DeepSeek open-sourced compiler tools, HPC libraries and communication software adapted for Ascend. The report is unconfirmed by either company. (read more).

A MindStudio write-up asks whether Linux is viable on Nvidia's new RTX Spark laptops, which are sold with Windows on ARM and up to 128GB unified memory for local AI work. Linux installs and runs with GPU acceleration through Nvidia's open kernel driver, but on wall power the GPU is locked at about 780 MHz instead of 2 GHz-plus on Windows. That cuts a 120-billion-parameter model from about 65 tokens per second to about 14, which the author ties to power-management firmware Linux cannot read. The install was not simple on an MSI Prestige: Omarchy gave a flickering display, Debian lacked drivers and Wi-Fi, and returning to Omarchy, which bundles Nvidia's open driver, worked. Linux first saw only 28GB of 128GB because firmware reserves 96GB for the GPU, changeable only from Windows. Lowering that reservation freed 120GB, after which 4K at 120Hz, audio, touchpad and CUDA worked. The takeaway for AI developers: usable, but you will lose about two-thirds of inference speed on Linux until the firmware issue is solved. (read more).

The research claim

An amateur says Claude Code and Codex helped find an exoplanet candidate; TESS will test it by Nov 26. Pharma leaders doubt the bigger AI-cures-disease promises.

The Wall Street Journal reports that pharmaceutical leaders doubt claims that AI could cure most diseases within a decade. No AI-designed drug has won FDA clearance, and pharma leaders say biology, not computing, is the obstacle. (read more 🔒).

Matthew Honnibal argues that the viral 'Astra can read and write weights to improve Qwen' post is misleading by blending different experiments under one headline. The abstract says 'just by looking at the raw weights' GPT-6 Astra can tell what a network was trained to do and can fix Qwen3.5-0.8B by editing 0.016% of weights, lifting MATH-500 from 7.9% to 48.1%. He says the raw-weights-in-context, no-tools setting appears only in experiments 1, 2 and 4, on tiny networks of 17K to 172K weights. Every experiment on a real language model uses Python, and the headline Qwen result also runs the model on up to 50 batches of practice problems. One footnote notes the game network beats PPO-500M only on Breakout and loses on Seaquest and Asterix. He compares it to a stage magician doing the same trick multiple ways while the audience assumes one technique. His conclusion: the Qwen result shows Astra can be prompted to do what a human ML engineer does with code, which is not nothing, but it does not show a single deep capability of reading weights directly. Experiments of different kinds should not share one framing. (read more).

Prime Intellect reports what it found auditing public evaluations quoted in model cards. While running popular benchmarks it hit issues that change how results should be read, and after fixes some benchmarks look saturated. It open-sources its fixes and describes design patterns and reward hacks seen when running frontier models. The example is Zapier's AutomationBench, where agents must complete business workflows across apps such as Gmail, Zoom and Asana by searching for actions and executing them. The discoverable actions described request bodies in prose instead of JSON Schema, so models often guessed at parameters and allowed combinations. Prime Intellect replaced the prose with JSON Schemas, closer to how models are used in production. The post continues with other benchmarks; only the opening and the first case were read. This Latent Space AINews issue admits the Weight-Space Programming claims were presented in an overly favorable light but says the finding is still a big wake-up moment with several independent replications, so it runs no retraction. It then summarizes the day's replications and caveats: Alex Krentsel's cross-generation replication found a sharp capability jump, with the latest models producing Breakout networks scoring up to 22x an RL baseline; Hugging Face reproduced the paper with its ML Intern harness; and a user had a model hand-write a 100K-parameter classifier for fake legal citations that reportedly beat a trained one. A Zhihu reconstruction of the Qwen edit says the gain is narrower than the headline: 576 of 579 repaired answers had hit the 32K-token limit because of looping, Astra located a 'repetition direction' mainly in layers 15 and 19 MLPs and subtracted it from top neurons, and held-out accuracy rose from 10.6% to 60.4%, mostly existing reasoning that could now finish. Simon Willison reports a laptop reproduction with Qwen 3.8 27B. The issue also features AI Engineer Shanghai and a history-of-Latent-Space interview, and is long; only the opening section was read. Pavel Rabtsevich, a 28-year-old product manager in Spain with no astronomy training or telescope, says he may have found an exoplanet by pointing AI tools at public TESS data. He gave measurements of over 126,000 stars to TypeSafe's Jev, Claude Code and OpenAI's Codex and had them look for periodic dimming, iterating on a candidate list. He stresses that he did not take first answers: he pushed the models to improve their methods, cross-checked between them, and decided which tests to run and what to keep. The candidate is TIC 4206066, a star a bit smaller than the Sun about 116 light-years away, dimming every 3.18 days. The same dips appear in the 2020 and 2018 data. He estimates a planet about 1.4 times Earth's size with a surface near 1,000 degrees Fahrenheit, and a possible second signal at 11.13 days he finds less convincing. He ran 74 tests with Claude Code trying to disprove it, including predicting the third dataset's dips from two, which worked in all three cases. He asked MIT to point TESS at it; the request was approved, with observations every two minutes from October 31 to November 26. He posted predicted dimming times and said he would post it publicly if it fails. This is a claim awaiting confirmation, not a discovery yet; the article is Gizmodo's, and only part of it was read. The godfather's warning

Hinton says AI might not have been worth building and warns of a major incident within two years.

In a Guardian interview, Geoffrey Hinton says it might turn out humanity would have been better off never developing AI, while insisting he is not a doomer and that AI could bring a 'wonderful future'. He warns of a possible large-scale incident this year or next, such as a temporary blackout of financial or power systems, and says that within about ten years we will know whether AI's risks can be managed, giving 'radical abundance' or 'massive unemployment' and a society torn apart. He was speaking before a screening of 'AI: Probably Nothing to Worry About', a documentary featuring him and Demis Hassabis; he says he feels a duty to use his reputation and will 'burn it all' on the cause. He singles out leaders: he does not trust Sam Altman 'at all', and says Zuckerberg, Musk and Altman understand the risks but would rather have the money. Altman said in September the world should trust that OpenAI will do the right thing. Hinton, 78, has worked on AI for about 44 years and left Google in 2023 to speak freely. (read more).

The number problem

The UK admits its 400 billion pound AI windfall figure came from a Google-commissioned report; its own estimate is under a third.

WIRED reports that the UK government told the public for two years that AI could add 400 billion pounds to the economy, but its AI minister, Kanishka Narayan, admitted in a parliamentary answer that the figure was far above the government's own projections. His own estimate is that AI adoption and data investment could add 55 to 140 billion pounds by 2030, with a central estimate of 120 billion, which he called 'hugely uncertain'. He said the 400 billion figure came from a 2023 public report commissioned by Google and produced by consultancy Public First, now called PF Global, so the government's own estimate was less than a third of the number it had used to calm fears about jobs and an economic crash. The security desk

Sentra raises $150M to govern what AI agents can read.

Sentra raised $150 million to expand its platform for controlling what AI agents can reach inside corporate data stores, SiliconANGLE reports; Capital One led the Series C per the headline. The company says its business grew more than 300% in a year. Its software works on data at rest wherever it is stored. Each customer gets classification tuned to its business, plus an identity map showing which people, service accounts and AI agents can reach a dataset, including inherited permissions. The pitch is that every agent switched on is another identity reading data, often around the clock, with no central control plane, and only a minority of organizations have agent governance. Co-founder and CEO Yoav Regev calls agents 'the busiest consumers' of data. Growth and market claims are the company's. (read more).

The open-weight race

A chip-design lab says it trained a US open-weight coding leader for $32M, and Tencent shows Hy4.

Tencent published a research page for Hy4 on hy.tencent.ai, shared on Hacker News as 'Tencent Hy4'. The page could not be read, so the model's details are unknown. (read more ⚠).

Ricursive Intelligence, a lab building self-improving systems by co-optimizing hardware, software and model for chip design, released Ripple-65B Preview, a general-purpose variant of the proprietary model behind its chip-design platform. It is a dense 65B model pretrained on 12 trillion tokens with up to 1M context, and the lab claims a new US open-weight state of the art on agentic coding: 80.52 on TerminalBench 2.1, ahead of larger US open models such as Reflection Beam 501B (80.1), Meta Muse Spark Open 816B (76.4), Thinking Machines Inkling 975B (63.8) and NVIDIA Nemotron 3 Ultra 550B (56.4). The cost claim is the headline: $32M of total R&D from scratch, including data mixing, evals, debugging, synthetic data and recipe development, which the lab says is one-tenth the cost of the previous open-weight leader. It contrasts that with Altman's $100M+ for GPT-4 and Amodei's projections of $10-100B. Rather than brute-force RL, it says it iterated on SFT and RL recipes for compute efficiency. The small dense design runs on local devices such as NVIDIA DGX Spark. Weights are on Hugging Face, Together AI, GMI Cloud and OpenRouter. All benchmark and cost figures are the lab's own. The agent shopper, measured

DoorDash sees only a few hundred orders from outside AI agents against a billion orders a quarter.

Ars Technica reports that almost no one is using outside AI agents to order on DoorDash. On the Q3 call, CEO Tony Xu said agents generated 'several hundred orders' over the time they have been live, a 'very low use case' for a company that handled nearly 1 billion orders in the quarter. Xu sees agents helping with long-consideration purchases and proactive background work, but said for something as simple as ordering food, people come directly to DoorDash. The article adds context: DoorDash's MCP connector launched less than a month ago, is limited to early beta partners and mostly used for corporate lunches, and without it agents must use the web interface. The figure excludes DoorDash's own AI tools. Its 'Ask DoorDash' feature builds baskets five times faster, with orders averaging nearly 50% higher basket value and about 60% more unique items, per the company. (read more).
