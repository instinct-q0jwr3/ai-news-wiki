The full renderer research keeps its tested scope

The Upload2Shell article behind yesterday's disclosure index separates demonstrated delegate chains from paths only traced to the renderer.

pwn.ai's research article by Paulos Yibelo details Upload2Shell, now CVE-2026-125396, a use-after-free in Ghostscript's Type 1 font loader that the authors say gave a write-what-where primitive and let a crafted document alter the state enforcing -dSAFER to run commands as the rendering process. The article explains how ordinary features such as preview generation or cropping handed files with trusted extensions to Ghostscript through ImageMagick delegates or pixel-decoding calls, describes a WordPress-shaped proof reaching Ghostscript through Imagick's readImage with PostScript bytes named as a PDF, and lists decoder tests in which PHP GD, OpenCV, prebuilt Sharp, Jimp, Java ImageIO and Go's standard decoders did not invoke Ghostscript. Its timeline says WordPress shipped its mitigation on 12 August, Artifex authored the fix on 2 September and released 10.08.0 on 8 September, a Debian network CUPS proof was completed on 17 September, and the CVE was published on 9 November, with a Debian 13 CUPS case study promised as the first full write-up. This is the research article behind the Upload2Shell disclosure index covered yesterday, not a separate vulnerability. The version matrix describes tested libraries rather than every product built on them, and reaching the renderer remains distinct from proven execution in any specific deployment, a distinction the authors themselves draw. Consensus blocks an autonomous-weapons mandate

A week of UN talks ends with a weaker instrument after objections attributed to Russia and reluctance from several military powers.

The Guardian reports that week-long efforts at the UN's Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons in Geneva to agree a negotiating mandate for a legally binding treaty on autonomous weapons collapsed after all-night talks failed to overcome Russian objections. Because the CCW operates by consensus among its 128 parties, the conference adopted a weaker option asking countries to elaborate an instrument by consensus, one that may not carry any legal obligations. The article says more than 100 states had pushed for a new law, with a bloc of Austria, Belgium, Brazil, Ireland, Mexico, New Zealand and Panama leading, while diplomats described Russia as filibustering and the US, South Korea, Japan, Turkey, the UK, France and Germany as reluctant to accept binding obligations. The report attributes claims of deliberate obstruction to unnamed diplomats and its account of AI-enabled targeting in Gaza and Iran to investigative media, neither independently verified here. A collapsed mandate is not a decision that no rules will ever exist, and the weaker instrument's eventual content was not settled by this meeting. A prize essay argues, and a hypothetical explores

Anil Seth's case against conscious AI and a panel's thought experiment about a machine proof are positions and speculation, not results.

Noema publishes Anil Seth's Berggruen Prize-winning essay arguing that consciousness is more likely a property of life than of computation, and that creating conscious or even conscious-seeming AI is a bad idea. Seth, a neuroscience professor at the University of Sussex, contends that identifying consciousness with intelligence and language makes people see it where it does not exist and miss it where it does, reviews cultural precedents from the Golem to Ex Machina, and criticizes computational functionalism by pointing to the biological brain's materiality. He notes that cognitive biases predispose people to overattribute consciousness to machines, and warns that granting rights to systems that merely seem conscious would hamper the ability to control or shut them down. This is a prize-winning philosophical essay, an argued position rather than a settled scientific consensus; thinkers named in the piece itself, such as David Chalmers and Geoffrey Hinton, hold more permissive views about machine consciousness. The essay's force is in its framing and its account of biological evidence, not in new experiments, and its policy conclusion about AI rights follows from premises that remain contested within consciousness science. One loop, every device

A hobby compiler project promises CPU-to-multi-GPU execution without a GPU toolkit, on its own measurements.

xc is a hobby heterogeneous data-parallel language project: write an ordinary loop in xc or C and its compiler, xcc, runs it across every CPU core, on GPUs through Metal, CUDA, Vulkan or WebGPU, across multiple GPUs from different vendors, or on a GPU in another machine, keeping whichever run is fastest. The project says kernels are generated by xcc itself and carried inside the executable, so a build needs no GPU toolkit installed. The repository holds the compiler plus a GUI framework and tools, and its README describes contribution rules, a contributor licence agreement and a clean-start history after earlier check-ins mixed in private material. The performance and portability claims are the project's own, with benchmarks the author says were measured on hardware the project bought; no figure was independently reproduced here, and results on one person's machines may not transfer to other GPUs or workloads. The multi-vendor and remote-GPU execution modes are described in the documentation, not demonstrated to this pass. The compiler was not built or run. (read more).
