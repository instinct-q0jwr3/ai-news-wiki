The full renderer research keeps its tested scope

The Upload2Shell article behind yesterday's disclosure index separates demonstrated delegate chains from paths only traced to the renderer.

pwn.ai's research article by Paulos Yibelo details Upload2Shell, now CVE-2026-125396, a use-after-free in Ghostscript's Type 1 font loader that the authors say gave a write-what-where primitive and let a crafted document alter the state enforcing -dSAFER to run commands as the rendering process. The article explains how ordinary features such as preview generation or cropping handed files with trusted extensions to Ghostscript through ImageMagick delegates or pixel-decoding calls, describes a WordPress-shaped proof reaching Ghostscript through Imagick's readImage with PostScript bytes named as a PDF, and lists decoder tests in which PHP GD, OpenCV, prebuilt Sharp, Jimp, Java ImageIO and Go's standard decoders did not invoke Ghostscript. Its timeline says WordPress shipped its mitigation on 12 August, Artifex authored the fix on 2 September and released 10.08.0 on 8 September, a Debian network CUPS proof was completed on 17 September, and the CVE was published on 9 November, with a Debian 13 CUPS case study promised as the first full write-up. This is the research article behind the Upload2Shell disclosure index covered yesterday, not a separate vulnerability. The version matrix describes tested libraries rather than every product built on them, and reaching the renderer remains distinct from proven execution in any specific deployment, a distinction the authors themselves draw. Consensus blocks an autonomous-weapons mandate

A week of UN talks ends with a weaker instrument after objections attributed to Russia and reluctance from several military powers.

The Guardian reports that week-long efforts at the UN's Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons in Geneva to agree a negotiating mandate for a legally binding treaty on autonomous weapons collapsed after all-night talks failed to overcome Russian objections. Because the CCW operates by consensus among its 128 parties, the conference adopted a weaker option asking countries to elaborate an instrument by consensus, one that may not carry any legal obligations. The article says more than 100 states had pushed for a new law, with a bloc of Austria, Belgium, Brazil, Ireland, Mexico, New Zealand and Panama leading, while diplomats described Russia as filibustering and the US, South Korea, Japan, Turkey, the UK, France and Germany as reluctant to accept binding obligations. Max Tegmark and Human Rights Watch's Bonnie Docherty are quoted criticizing the outcome after thirteen years of discussion. The report attributes claims of deliberate obstruction to unnamed diplomats and its account of AI-enabled targeting in Gaza and Iran to investigative media, neither independently verified here. A collapsed mandate is not a decision that no rules will ever exist, and the weaker instrument's eventual content was not settled by this meeting. The article is one news organization's account of closed negotiations; no primary conference documents were inspected. (read more).

A prize essay argues, and a hypothetical explores

Anil Seth's case against conscious AI and a panel's thought experiment about a machine proof are positions and speculation, not results.

Noema publishes Anil Seth's Berggruen Prize-winning essay arguing that consciousness is more likely a property of life than of computation, and that creating conscious or even conscious-seeming AI is a bad idea. Seth, a neuroscience professor at the University of Sussex, contends that identifying consciousness with intelligence and language makes people see it where it does not exist and miss it where it does, reviews cultural precedents from the Golem to Ex Machina, and criticizes computational functionalism by pointing to the biological brain's materiality. He notes that cognitive biases predispose people to overattribute consciousness to machines, and warns that granting rights to systems that merely seem conscious would hamper the ability to control or shut them down. This is a prize-winning philosophical essay, an argued position rather than a settled scientific consensus; thinkers named in the piece itself, such as David Chalmers and Geoffrey Hinton, hold more permissive views about machine consciousness. The essay's force is in its framing and its account of biological evidence, not in new experiments, and its policy conclusion about AI rights follows from premises that remain contested within consciousness science. A video essay asks a series of mathematicians and scientists the hypothetical question of what would happen if an AI solved the Riemann Hypothesis. The speakers distinguish an inscrutable machine-generated proof, perhaps a Lean certificate billions of lines long checked by multiple kernels, from a beautiful conceptual proof that advances understanding; several say a verified but unreadable proof would be valuable yet would set off a community effort to comprehend and re-exposit it rather than ending the subject. The four color theorem is cited as a brute-force precedent, one speaker calls a machine proof of a Millennium problem a possible Move 37 moment for mathematics, and the discussion touches on what gets commoditized when problem solving becomes cheap and on whether superhuman provers would also become superhuman explainers. A legal corpus publishes what it does not hold

Pandex pairs its European law API and MCP server with an unusually explicit register of coverage gaps, all self-reported.

Pandex presents itself as a single API and MCP server for European law: national legislation with past versions and the decisions of the highest courts of the EU and seven countries, grounded in the institutions that publish them, with citations attached to every answer. The landing page lists document counts per jurisdiction and, unusually, publishes the gaps it knows about: French decisions served only through Judilibre need a registered account there and the official dumps run about two months behind, older Dutch decisions have no text at Rechtspraak, and several court or treaty sites answer its server with Cloudflare or proof-of-work challenges. Pricing is listed as free during the beta, one euro per thousand requests for products, and a corpus license from 1,000 euros a month, with a fixed two-year rate for the first ten customers. All figures come from the vendor's own page, dated 17 November 2026 for its institution register, and were not audited against the source institutions. Coverage counts describe what the company holds, not the completeness of any national archive, and the beta pricing is an offer that can change rather than a stable quote. The piece collects anecdotes: a cybersecurity founder who hired a matchmaker partly out of fear of spies, an anonymous tech worker who rates his espionage anxiety at seven out of ten, and a Palantir employee who said he would not date Chinese or Russian nationals. The article is built from matchmaker and consultant anecdotes, not prevalence data, and one San Francisco matchmaker says she has not heard more honeypot concerns, only broader dating skepticism.
