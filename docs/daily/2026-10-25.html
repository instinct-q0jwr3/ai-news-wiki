Creative work under the machine

Apricitas counts 200,000 US creative-industry jobs lost in four years - recession-grade losses with no recession - and asks how much of it is AI.

Joseph Politano (Apricitas) documents that America has lost more than 200,000 jobs in creative industries over the last four years, roughly 50,000 in the last year alone - a stretch of media job losses comparable in intensity and duration only to the 2001 and 2008 recessions, except there is no generalized recession now. The losses coincide with the rise of AI systems that mass-produce text, images, music and other digital art at extremely low cost, prompting the question of whether this is the fall of the Creative Class. Proving the AI share of the losses is hard: media firms are coy about AI use (partly to protect the legal basis of their copyrights), and consolidation, offshoring and the shift to social-media creators all confound the picture. The clearest signal is breadth: nearly every digital-arts subsector is losing jobs while in-person entertainment keeps growing. Hollywood is the worst hit - movie and sound recording has shed over 100,000 jobs, nearly a third of the sector, touching employment lows approaching 30-year bottoms - and accounts for almost half the four-year media losses; written publishing is next at over 70,000. Losses run at a steady ~50k per year and have accelerated recently. Politano frames the moment against the first digital transition - the internet era that began with Metallica's Napster problem - to ask what AI will do to the business of art. (read more).

Websites learn to talk to agents

A static-blog author ships a full recipe for agent-readable publishing: markdown mirrors, content negotiation, llms.txt and explicit crawler signals.

The author of the Deallocated Objects blog spent an evening making the site readable for AI agents (Claude, ChatGPT, Perplexity and friends), and the write-up is a practical recipe for any static site. Every post now also exists as plain markdown via an .md suffix, with YAML frontmatter carrying title, canonical URL, author, dates and description. The neater trick is content negotiation: an nginx map rewrites to the .md file when the Accept header asks for text/markdown, with Vary: Accept so caches never mix the two, and Link canonical plus X-Robots-Tag: noindex on the markdown so search engines don't see duplicates. The site also publishes /llms.txt (a markdown index explaining what the site is, how to get markdown, citation preferences and a one-line summary of every post), /llms-full.txt (all posts in one 225 KB file) and /index.md, with a Link header on every response pointing at llms.txt. robots.txt explicitly allows the major AI crawlers and adds a Content-Signal line (search=yes, ai-input=yes, ai-train=yes) - the author wants to be cited, while noting a product site might choose differently. Feeds got markdown alternates too (Atom summary links and a JSON Feed _markdown_url). The pass also fixed inherited issues: rebuilt descriptions from rendered text, removed maximum-scale=1 so phones can pinch-zoom, and added og:image. (read more).

Agents enter the clinic

Baba raises $40M to put AI care teams in front of Medicare and Medicaid patients, with a benchmark for long-horizon patient advocacy in tow.

Baba, a 2025-founded care platform that connects Medicare and Medicaid patients to dedicated care teams (care coordinators and licensed therapists), raised a $40M Series A led by FirstMark Capital, with CVS Health Ventures and Valtruis participating and General Catalyst, Genius Ventures and Ground Up Ventures returning. FirstMark's Rick Heitzmann frames it as a bet on the consumerization of healthcare for rural, older and complex-need patients. Baba says it supports more than 10,000 patients with 100,000 families on its waiting list, and CMS selected it for the 10-year ACCESS model. The money goes to research, reaching more health plans and accelerating value-based arrangements; CEO Connor Sweeney says the goal is a model where Baba takes on risk for coordinated, accountable care. The company also launched BabaBench, a benchmark measuring frontier AI agents on long-horizon patient advocacy tasks like paperwork, billing and insurer calls. (read more).

Kimi's crown comes with questions

VentureBeat's assessment finds Kimi K3.1 Pro's agentic benchmarks genuinely historic but its reasoning gains modest, its self-hosting story unfinished, and its launch shadowed by unverified but ugly allegations of an undisclosed paid influencer campaign.

VentureBeat's deep dive on Kimi K3.1 Pro frames it as a likely historic open-weights release - it already outscores Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.6-Pro, DeepSeek V4.1-Flash, Meta's Muse Wonder and OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra on Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index, and should take the open-weights crown when weights ship November 4. The biggest independent gains are in agentic, multi-step work: Terminal-Bench 4.0 jumps from 13% to 67% (ahead of Sonnet 5.5's 64%), CyberGym-E2E from 58% to 95%, Cyber Index from 41% to 64%, with smaller gains on pure reasoning (Humanity's Last Exam 47% to 52%). Moonshot's own comparisons tell a more mixed story - Kimi trails Opus and Astra badly on Terminal-Bench Science - so the evidence points to improved task execution rather than an across-the-board reasoning leap. API pricing is $2/$10 per million input/output tokens (a third below K3, half of Opus 5.5), with a Highspeed tier at $4/$20 claiming 2-3x speed; DeepSeek and Xiaomi remain far cheaper, and self-hosting waits on the November weights, whose license terms are still unknown. Moonshot's API runs from Singapore-based servers, a data-location consideration given the DHS order excluding DeepSeek from US federal procurement. The launch has also drawn scrutiny over an alleged paid influencer campaign on X. User Elshayib posted screenshots of what he described as Kimi promotional instructions, which he says he declined; they required draft approval and directed creators not to use the paid-partnership label - a potential violation of FTC disclosure guidance and X policy. Pekingnology's reporting notes the document is not independently authenticated: it is unclear whether it came from Moonshot, an agency or another party, how many creators received it, or whether any undisclosed endorsements ran. VentureBeat stresses the allegations do not invalidate the independent benchmark results, but they do underline the need to separate promotional enthusiasm from reproducible evidence. (read more).

Contests, bans and other collisions

Nikon strips a microscopy prize over an AI-generated entry, and a viral thread turns an OpenAI ban for reverse engineering into a parable about economic dependence on platform AI.

The winner of Nikon's 16th Small World in Motion contest, one of the most prestigious scientific photography awards, was stripped of his title after researchers concluded his entry was AI-generated. Dr. Ning Xu of Tsinghua University had won with a video of cilia beating in the airways of a child with primary ciliary dyskinesia. Scientists flagged structures that 'pop in and out of existence' and cilia that 'appear from nowhere,' and a UT Southwestern PhD student found what appeared to be an AI-generation watermark in the source image; two former judges also raised concerns. In a now-deleted LinkedIn post, Xu admitted using AI in post-processing but insisted it only helped distinguish and visualize features in reconstructed grayscale images, not to generate the video, cilia or their movement, and that he broke no rules. Skeptics noted the explanation doesn't account for the abnormalities, and that sharing the RAW grayscale video could have settled the question. Nikon's investigation found a rules violation, awarded the top prize to Vietnam's Nguyen Nam Nhat (a roundworm video), and said it will revisit rules and evaluation procedures. The piece situates the case in a years-long run of AI-generated entries winning human competitions, from the 2022 Colorado State Fair to a 2023 digiDirect photo contest. (read more).

The specialists strike back

A decade-old CNN fine-tuned in twenty minutes beats two frontier LLMs at skin-cancer diagnosis, an open-source TTS model serves the accents Big Tech flattens, and a hobbyist coaxes SIMD computer vision out of a $2 microcontroller.

A hobbyist has coaxed serious computer-vision performance out of the ESP32-S3, a dual-core 240MHz microcontroller that costs about two dollars, by exploiting a set of 128-bit SIMD instructions buried in its technical reference manual. The project is a SIMD-accelerated implementation of the FAST corner detector, the first stage of many feature-matching pipelines, and it runs roughly twice as fast as the reference implementation, processing a QVGA frame in about six milliseconds. The write-up doubles as a tutorial on the chip's eccentricities. Finding almost no example code online, the author worked from three scattered sources, learned the ESP32-S3's assembly conventions, and hit its unusual constraints - including alignment rules and a quirky instruction set - head on. Along the way the project spawned basm, a small custom register allocator written to make the hand-written vector code manageable. The result is both a practical detector for cheap vision hardware and a readable map of an instruction set almost nobody documents. (read more).

A clinician's head-to-head test has a humbling result for frontier AI: a fine-tuned copy of Inception v3, a convolutional network Google published in 2015, beat both GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 at diagnosing skin cancer from images. On 200 biopsy-proven lesions from the public MILK10k dataset, the CNN named the correct diagnosis among six options for 125 cases (62%), against 84 and 85 (about 42%) for the two flagship LLMs - despite being fine-tuned for only about twenty minutes on a single H100. The test was designed to be clinically honest. Every lesion was biopsied, including the benign ones, so the set skews toward cases that worried a real clinician; each lesion came with both a clinical photo and a dermoscopic image. The LLMs were prompted as a clinician might use them - most likely diagnosis, confidence, second choice, and whether the patient could be safely discharged - and both would have discharged at least one melanoma as a harmless seborrhoeic keratosis. The CNN was deliberately not state of the art: pretrained on everyday photographs, fine-tuned on 68,011 ISIC Archive images with all test lesions and same-patient photos excluded. The author's conclusion is that for narrow, high-stakes visual classification, a cheap specialist model still outperforms general-purpose giants that cost orders of magnitude more. (read more).

Destined AI has released Hurston-1, an open-source text-to-speech model built for the accents most TTS systems flatten into generic American English: Southern American, Nigerian, West African, East African and Southern African, alongside broader American English. It is a fine-tune of the Apache-2.0 Maya1 model and the first release in the company's ClarityVoice family, steered not by fixed voice presets but by plain-language descriptions of the voice wanted. The headline feature is NB-TTS, narrowband text-to-speech for the phone line, where most customer service still happens, including calls answered by AI agents. Phone mode emits 8 kHz G.711 audio ready for Twilio and other telephony systems, trained in part on real calls between the company's paid, consented contributors and AI voice agents; full 24 kHz output remains the default. The Atlanta team, recording contributors across the American South and Africa since 2021, says it built the model without outside funding and named it for Zora Neale Hurston, the anthropologist who documented how Black Southerners actually spoke. Homework, data plumbing and the iPhone

A Fields medalist at Berkeley stops grading homework because AI has made it meaningless, Sunnny pitches a cleaned customer-data layer built for AI consumption, and Gruber and Stern talk iPhone on The Talk Show.

Sunnny is a new B2B product that consolidates a company's scattered customer data into one cleaned, identity-resolved view designed to be read by people and AI models alike. The pitch: valuable customer information sits fragmented across CRMs and other systems, and Sunnny ingests it, cleans it, resolves identities and relationships, and presents the result neatly. Rather than a slide deck, the founders - Mikkel and Mike, a small mostly-remote Bay Area team - are launching with a live demo populated with simulated data that visitors can explore directly. Prospective customers who want to try their own data are onboarded by hand for now, with a wider release planned before the end of the year. The company emphasizes that customer data stays the customer's: it is not read, trained on, or shared, and users can manage their own encryption keys. Standard connectors cover popular CRMs, plus a universal connector, and the team is developing skills and instructions so AI models can connect to systems and populate Sunnny directly. (read more).

Richard Borcherds, a UC Berkeley mathematics professor and Fields medalist, has stopped grading his students' homework for correctness. With AI tools reliably producing top-scoring answers, he told the student newspaper there was no point paying readers to grade work that AI now completes by default; he marks for completion instead, a system he calls not very good but the only one available. The interview lands days after OpenAI said its agents had apparently solved the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of mathematics' most famous open questions. Borcherds says that if AI keeps improving at its present rate, within a few years there will be nothing human mathematicians can do that AI cannot do better - a reversal from five years ago, when mathematics looked like one of the careers safest from automation. He no longer knows whether to advise students to enter the field, describes deep institutional inertia in academia, comparing journals to print publications that outlived their usefulness, and frames the moment as mathematics' first encounter with the kind of displacement that looms brought to weaving.
