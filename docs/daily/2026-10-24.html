Odd drops

An agent multiplexer for your terminal, a B-tree heresy about disk speed, Doctorow on why fixing others' stories teaches writing, a one-prompt photo painter, and a $1B AI film studio.

'Memory is slow, Disk is fast' argues that Big O notation lies by omission: an O(sqrt n) disk-based structure can beat an O(log n) one in practice when constants and I/O patterns favor it. The author built a disk-backed ordered structure whose directory blocks split as they grow - and discloses the corner case honestly: the top-level index grows with n, so on average once every couple hundred thousand inserts it memmoves about 1.4 MiB and rebuilds its count tree, which is noise next to drive I/O. Had the author made the index recursive as planned - extents all the way up - the structure would officially be a B-tree variant with guaranteed O(log n); at current settings a second tier only kicks in around 8G nodes. The point stands: asymptotic classes mislead when the machine's real costs are block transfers and cache misses, and a well-shaped disk layout can make the 'slow' medium look fast. Source code is published. (read more).

Cory Doctorow's Pluralistic links post for October 23 leads with 'Critique and writing': the argument that the most important part of a writing workshop is fixing other people's stories, not receiving notes on your own. Peer workshops work for counter-intuitive reasons - the obvious one is that they make you write at all, replacing impossible self-discipline with a group's expectation of regular new work. The deeper mechanism: critiquing others' drafts trains the exact judgment your own blind spots resist - it is easier to see a story's flaws when your ego isn't attached to it, and that diagnostic skill transfers back to your own pages. The post rounds out with his regular 'Hey look at this' delights and an 'Object permanence' item on the USPS. (read more).

Meta's updated Commercial Terms, due to take effect on October 30, transfer legal responsibility for mistakes made by its AI features to enterprise customers, including changes made by features Meta switches on itself. The new 'AI Features' section states that customers are solely responsible for all risks from AI outputs, a shift from the current terms where liability attached only when advertisers chose to use the AI tools. The payoff question gets harder

Apollo's Torsten Slok finds zero AI payoff outside tech - flat revenue per worker and margins across the S&P 493 - while Gary Marcus totes up the harm ledger and the New York Times finds Alpha School's 2x-learning claim missing for half its K-8 students in math.

Reading the machine's output

A study pins down why AI prose is getting harder to parse, DeepMind's DetectorScope makes AI text detectors show their evidence, and mathematicians organize - controversially - to revise OpenAI's opaque math write-ups.

An arXiv study investigates the widespread sense that AI-generated text has become harder to understand, testing psycholinguistic features known to correlate with human processing difficulty: object relative clauses, long syntactic dependencies, low-frequency n-grams and similar. Across a new corpus of AI text in diverse domains, all nine frontier and near-frontier models use more inanimate subjects with animate verbs and more low-frequency n-grams than human writing, and most also produce longer dependencies, more stacked nouns and more reduced object relatives in conversation, with substantial variation across model families. The authors propose an explanation: human language is optimized for successful communication, while AI text is increasingly optimized for other goals - math, code, reasoning - where the pressure is information density rather than readability. As a proof of concept, they fine-tune a model under a length penalty and show its linguistic behavior diverges from human-like patterns. The paper frames this as a measurable axis of misalignment between human processing and LLM output, and points toward remedies. (read more).

On Terence Tao's blog, four mathematicians (Abhinav Nellore, Johannes Schmitt, Soledad Villar and Rachel Ward) announce oai-math-revised, a community hub that aims to improve OpenAI's math write-ups, which they describe as difficult to understand and carrying incomplete citations. The GitHub repository invites the broader math community - students, postdocs, professors, enthusiasts - to contribute revisions, and asks for volunteers to serve as admins and reviewers for the categories in OpenAI's overview document. The comment section immediately pushes back. Several commenters question why unpaid academics should polish the output of a trillion-dollar for-profit company, arguing the project validates OpenAI's approach, pigeonholes mathematicians into interpreting the machine's gifts, and will simply feed better training data back to the lab without acknowledgment. One suggests OpenAI should pay for the service; another notes that several organizers work in AI-for-math, so the effort is not a neutral community body. (read more).

DetectorScope, a Google DeepMind project, turns a black-box AI text detector into an auditable one whose every prediction decomposes into named, validated features with locations in the text. Given a sentence, it reports not just a label and probability but the evidence: for example, that 'the word about used as a preposition' pushed the logit up by 0.23 while a 'conversational filler phrase' pushed it down by 0.05, with each contribution traceable to token positions. The pipeline is a concept bottleneck over sparse autoencoder features: SAEs encode the detector model's residual stream, each feature gets a natural-language description validated against its own activations, and a sparse linear head over the validated features makes the final call, so the logit literally equals bias plus the sum of feature importances. A tau parameter (default 0.85) sets the interpretability threshold: higher tau admits fewer, better-described features and more legible evidence at some accuracy cost. Across COLING and RAID benchmarks the auditable detectors stay close to the fine-tuned black-box classifier they are built on, matching it even at thresholds of 0.7 and above. Released on PyPI and Hugging Face with a demo. (read more).

Robots and agents learn new tricks

Dream Labs exits stealth with $90M and a robot model that works on unseen hardware out of the box, Skyvern makes its browser agent 2.3x faster by deleting its RAG layer, and Apple's iPhone Duo gets a focal-plane-bending open transition.

Apple human interface designer Chan Karunamuni, cited by Daring Fireball, describes the open/close transition he designed for the newly announced iPhone Duo. The animation aims to make the device's two displays feel like one seamless surface by perceptually bending the focal plane rather than the content, so opening the phone feels like opening a window onto a single view. (read more ⚠).

Dream Labs came out of stealth with $90M in seed funding and launched Dream-1, a World Action Model for bimanual robots available through a public API. The claim: connect a new two-armed robot with parallel grippers and it performs tasks out of the box - no fine-tuning, no demonstrations from that robot, no ML expertise required. The company frames this as early zero-shot embodiment generalization, and the first crack in robotics' chicken-and-egg problem: a robot needs to be useful to sell, but usefulness required data from that exact robot, which only exists once enough units are in the field. The argument rests on two curves crossing. Hardware is collapsing in price: a capable bimanual arm setup now costs around $2,000, seven semi-humanoid robots sell for under $15,000, and Hugging Face's $399 Microduck sold 10,000+ units in four days - evidence that consumers, unlike spreadsheet-driven enterprises, will buy robots that are merely delightful. On the model side, Dream Labs' February DreamZero paper introduced World Action Models, and the team found that 30 minutes of data from a given robot was enough to transfer general-purpose task knowledge to it. Dream-1 extends that to zero-shot control of unseen robots on unseen tasks in unseen environments, with demo rollouts of sorting, stacking and folding. The bet: cheap hardware puts robots in homes, generalizable models make them useful immediately, and useful robots generate the data that makes the next model better. (read more).

Skyvern rewrote its open-source browser-automation agent, replacing its RAG-style architecture with progressive disclosure inspired by Pi, and made it 2.3x faster while scoring 90.5% on the Odysseys benchmark. The original design loaded each website, screenshotted and annotated it, condensed it into a list of elements and handed that to the LLM. That worked only if element identification was perfect, and the web is messy: Shadow DOMs, iframes, select2 dropdowns, Kendo widgets and canvas elements turned the utility into an endless DOM-interpreter project. Worse, agent performance stopped improving with better LLMs because it was bottlenecked by the parsing layer. The new architecture starts the agent with minimal context and gives it tools to pull more when needed - viable now because instruction following has been reliable since the Opus 4.5 generation of models. The team expected a trade: better accuracy in exchange for more calls, cost and latency. Instead, a production A/B test over roughly 100,000 runs showed the new design was faster and cheaper as well as more accurate; cached tokens carrying a 70% discount explain the net cost reduction despite higher token usage. Skyvern 3.0 topped the Odysseys benchmark with a 90.5% perfect rubric success rate at 65.4 average steps. The team still sees headroom in all three dimensions and is exploring fast 'system one' models like Jev, though current versions are limited by 64K context and no image processing. (read more).
