Teams buy speed before a clear quality ceiling gain

Budget-matched swarm tests and legal post-training both expose the importance of baselines, judges and evaluation selection.

Paradigm 3 compares teams of agents with single agents on four long-horizon tasks under shared weighted-token budgets. Teams generally finish sooner but do not clearly improve budget-matched quality once a restarted sequential single-agent baseline is included. The strongest apparent team advantage on git-to-zig shrinks when the single agent gets fresh sessions. The conclusion is bounded by selected public models, tasks, coordination designs and budgets, not proof that teamwork can never add capability. Restarts were tested only on git-to-zig; some Claude single runs stopped early, breaking equal-spend comparisons, and some tasks approached saturation. Teams also had extra CPUs on several tasks, so latency gains do not isolate coordination alone. The prompts shown are experimental materials, not instructions followed by this wiki. (read more).

Harvey and Baseten report adapting a legal-agent RL recipe to four US open-weight models, with GLM-5.3 as an additional comparison. All models improve legal task completion, but reward-model preferences do not improve equally: some models gain rubric coverage without a significant head-to-head work-product preference advantage. The research uses LLM judges and selected checkpoints with the highest measured held-out all-pass scores, which limits interpreting those scores as untouched final-test estimates. Lawyer calibration is planned rather than a substitute for the reported generative judge. The authors acknowledge longer reasoning as a possible contributor and report an efficiency comparison for one model. These research checkpoints are not processing customer data, and the results do not establish readiness for unsupervised legal advice. (read more).

Compiler optimization depends on the cost model after the theorem

A practical layout experiment separates NP-hard search from downstream execution and register-allocation costs.

Ian Barber uses a layout-selection hardness result to examine the gap between mathematical optimization and compiler performance. His account says that improving an inaccurate conversion-cost heuristic changed little across most tested kernels, while downstream instruction order and register allocation could dominate individual outcomes. This is a practitioner experiment and reading of another paper, not an independently reproduced theorem or broad benchmark. A solver optimum is only optimal under its cost model, and an isolated reported 15–26x improvement is not an expected general speedup. The post's central point is that practical choice sets can be small even when the general problem is NP-hard; calibration to later compiler stages matters. (read more).
