An automated assurance is not yet an adjudicated obligation

A reported eviction after AI-attributed rent emails raises disclosure and liability questions that remain factually and legally open.

A Substack report describes a North Carolina tenant, Laura Mack, who was evicted on 19 November after emails signed by her apartment complex appeared to accept a 30 November payment date for nearly $4,000 in overdue rent. She says a leasing employee later attributed the exchange to an AI assistant named Olivia, without disclosure in the emails themselves. The account relies on emails reviewed by local broadcaster WRAL and the tenant's statements; the property has not publicly confirmed that an AI assistant sent the messages or whether it was authorized to approve extensions. Whether the emails formed an enforceable agreement is unresolved, and the cited Air Canada chatbot ruling is a different jurisdiction, not legal precedent here. A state senator's disclosure bill has not advanced. This is a reported dispute, not an adjudicated finding about AI liability. (read more).

In-browser inference trades prompt speed for locality

An experimental WebAssembly engine approaches native generation speed on one machine while lagging badly on prompt processing.

Tesseracted describes an experimental terminal that runs Qwen-family models entirely in a browser tab: weights download once into cache and a roughly 52 KB WebAssembly module computes every token on the CPU, with no GPU code or inference API. The author measures generation reaching roughly 65-75% of native llama.cpp speed on an Apple M3 Max. Figures are the author's own measurements on one machine over two days, not an independent benchmark. Prompt processing is far slower than native because it is done token by token, and the Qwen3.5 models were not benchmarked against llama.cpp at all. The page's privacy claim that nothing leaves the tab was not verified by inspecting network behavior. No code was run and no model was downloaded here. (read more).

Export controls can strand the technology they guard

A lithography veteran argues the MATCH Act could hand serviced, reverse-engineerable scanners to rivals.

A lithography engineer with thirty years in the field argues that the proposed MATCH Act could backfire: banning sales and servicing of immersion scanners to China would strand roughly 350 ASML tools there, exposing their technology to rivals who could service, reverse-engineer and hire around them.
