Proactive agents move into products

Anthropic, GM and Meta all pushed agents that initiate rather than wait: Claude Duet opened a research preview as a background agent app, GM announced a voice-first OnStar assistant for the 2027 Lyriq, and Zuckerberg put early commerce numbers on Meta's Muse.

Anthropic has opened a research preview of Claude Duet, a standalone app where Claude acts as a proactive agent rather than a reactive chatbot. Open rails for running agents

Omnara launched an open-source, self-hostable alternative to the hosted agent APIs, while AuxoAI raised $20.7M to productize forward-deployed agent engineering for enterprises.

AuxoAI, an enterprise engineering and consulting company that helps large businesses build and deploy AI systems, has raised a $20.7 million Series A led by NTTVC to scale its consulting model. Over three-plus years working with Global 2000 enterprises, the company has built a practice around embedding on-site engineers and strategy teams inside clients to understand their culture, data, workflows, and customer engagement before building tailored AI solutions. Its platform centerpiece is Damia, described as a forward-deployed-engineer workbench that turns captured business knowledge and processes into reusable agent capabilities, applications, and licenses. The thesis is that the knowledge consultants accumulate while working with a customer should be preserved in software rather than walked out the door, so AI agents built around a company's processes keep improving. The raise funds growth and scaling of this FDE-plus-platform approach, a model in the mold of Palantir-style forward deployment now being productized for the agent era. (read more).

Omnara is an open-source, self-hostable platform for running durable AI agents, positioned as an alternative to Anthropic's Claude Managed Agents and OpenAI's Agents API. Instead of tying the agent loop to a vendor cloud, Omnara exposes an API where a call creates an agent, and the platform handles the loop, sandboxes for command execution, secrets, permissions, RBAC, and streaming. Because each agent's state lives in Postgres, agents survive restarts and cost nothing while idle, and users can message them and stream their events over the API. The project ships under Apache 2.0 and can also be used via Omnara Cloud, whose starter project comes preconfigured with an OpenRouter model provider and a pool of managed sandboxes, so a minimal agent.yaml runs out of the box. The launch post walks through the CLI flow (npx omnara agents launch with a declarative YAML config), terminal chat and a web dashboard for watching tool calls, and a TypeScript SDK for embedding agents in applications. The team previously built a remote-control phone app for Claude Code and Codex before rebuilding Omnara as a full managed-agent API. (read more).

Teaching agents to cooperate

A 2v2 Pokemon Showdown testbed measures how LLM teammates negotiate, correct each other and plan turns together.

Oliver Grabner built a multi-agent testbed on Pokemon Showdown's 2v2 doubles format to measure how well LLM agents coordinate, communicate, and negotiate actions as teammates. Most multi-agent research evaluates pipelines where agents hand tasks down a workflow; this environment instead places two LLM agents on the same side of a game-theoretic battle with hidden information and random damage, forcing them to read the opposing team, split targets, and plan around each other in natural-language team chat. The setup differs from the PokeAgent Challenge, where a single agent plays each side and specialist RL agents still outplay general LLMs, because it specifically tests coordination between teammates rather than individual play. Grabner's goal is a controlled environment for measuring collaborative behavior that could later serve as an RL training ground for encouraging it. Transcripts show agents correcting each other's type-matchup reasoning and agreeing on concrete turn plans, suggesting general LLMs already exhibit useful negotiation patterns, while the codebase is public on GitHub for others to extend. The grid under the build-out

A HARC report warns planned Texas data centers could erase decades of air-quality gains within five years if the load lands on gas.

A report from the Houston Advanced Research Center (HARC) warns that the data-center build-out in Texas could reverse decades of air-quality progress in as little as five years. Texas has become ground zero for the physical infrastructure behind generative AI, and new and proposed data centers are demanding enormous amounts of electricity. If all planned projects connect to the grid and draw power from the anticipated generation mix, pollution from the power sector would skyrocket. In that scenario, fine-particle soot would more than triple to its highest level since 2006, smog-forming NOx emissions would rise 136 percent toward 2004 levels, and carbon dioxide pollution would climb about 90 percent. The outcome hinges on how developers and utilities meet the demand: on-site gas turbines lock in the dirty path, while renewable generation would blunt much of the damage. Tech companies are already building their own power sources, and the report's authors caution that without cleaner choices, children and grandchildren could breathe dirtier air than today's adults. This guide shows how to embed the EYG scripting language inside an Erlang application, giving the host program a controlled scripting environment where access to capabilities is tightly governed. The walkthrough builds a minimal Erlang module, my_counters_script, that parses EYG source with eyg@parser:all_from_string and executes it with the interpreter, returning results to the Erlang shell. The appeal for Erlang and BEAM hosts is that EYG scripts run under explicit control of what they can reach, making the pattern suitable for exposing safe user scripting on top of a long-running system. Two AI empires

In a Foreign Affairs essay, Niall Ferguson, Lisa Klaassen, and Eric Schmidt argue that AI is an inherently imperial technology: by controlling the models, chips, infrastructure, and capital behind advanced machine cognition, the United States and China have each assembled a new kind of empire whose dominion extends over knowledge itself. Amrut Joshi argues that asking an LLM for a confidence score is a category error: the number it returns has no good reason to be trusted. Real confidence, like a weather forecaster's 70 percent chance of rain, is grounded in information - current observations and base rates from years of records - and calibrated systems are right about as often as their stated confidence. An LLM's self-reported score is not derived from any such evidence; it is another piece of generated text, shaped by how such statements usually read in training data. The post walks through why the failure is structural rather than a prompting problem: the model has no access to the accuracy distribution of its own answers, so the score cannot track correctness, and agent designers who gate actions on it are building on noise. The practical takeaway for agent builders is to derive confidence from things outside the model's narration - verification checks, retrieval agreement, tool results, or measured task outcomes - rather than from the model's own stated certainty. Models shrink toward the device

Recursive's Flint packs agentic coding into a 2.5B-active MoE for laptops, a 2B model now drives Android apps on the phone's own NPU, and Ant Group and vLLM open-sourced Humming to make quantized serving fast across GPUs.

Recursive introduced Flint, a mixture-of-experts model built for fast agentic coding on local hardware. Flint has 40 billion total parameters but activates just 2.5 billion per token, and the small team - people and agents working together - built it from scratch in under four months. The company claims Flint leads all MoE models under 50 billion total parameters on agentic coding benchmarks including SWE-Bench Pro v2, Terminal-Bench 2.1, and SWE-Bench Verified, while rivaling the coding performance of dense models in the 25-40 billion parameter range. The pitch is local-first: frontier-ish coding assistance that runs on a laptop, with the speed that a tiny active-parameter count buys. (read more).

Erik is an Android app that brings computer-use agents onto the phone itself: it reads the screen through the accessibility service and can tap and type in any other app. Claude, ChatGPT, or Codex can drive it from a computer, but when none is connected, a 2-billion-parameter model - GUI-Owl 1.5 2B in 4-bit quantization on llama.cpp - runs the same loop entirely on-device with no server involved. The engineering notes are striking: on a Mac M2 CPU with 4 threads, one agent step takes 7.3 seconds (down from 10.6), half of it the vision encoder; on a Galaxy S25's NPU that encoder runs in 236 ms versus 3.9 s on the M2. GUI-Owl 1.5, from Alibaba's Mobile-Agent team, is a Qwen3-VL fine-tune that maps a screenshot to a single action. It runs in the app via llama.cpp at 4 bits - 1.05 GB for the language model and 445 MB for the vision encoder, both kept resident between steps - with accuracy holding on a 36-screen test set. (read more).

Ant Group and the vLLM community are open-sourcing Humming, a general-purpose, high-performance kernel library for quantized inference. The motivation is the memory wall: as models and inference workloads grow, memory capacity, bandwidth, and compute costs constrain deployment, especially for MoE models like DeepSeek-V3, which stores 671 billion parameters while activating only 37 billion per token. Quantization cuts weight and activation precision to shrink memory use and data movement. Humming targets the fragmentation problem in quantized inference: configurations such as WNA16, W8A8, W4A8, and W4A4 mix weight and activation bit widths, and even at the same precision, schemes like INT4 vary across GPU generations. The library provides fast, flexible kernels across these configurations under the vLLM project, aiming to make low-precision serving practical on diverse hardware. (read more).

Supply chain under attack

LiteLLM's poisoned v1.107.0 release put credential-stealing malware on PyPI and Docker Hub, and Gary Marcus warns banks are already taking hits from AI-enabled attacks while defenses shrink.

LiteLLM's release pipeline was compromised in the early morning of October 21, pushing version 1.107.0 to the Docker registry and PyPI with compiled malware that steals credentials and loads remote-code execution tools. All previous versions are safe, the impacted release has been pulled, and enterprise customers on the private release were not affected. The maintainers label the incident contained, with the investigation ongoing. In a 20:30 UTC update, the team said they believe they have identified the attack chain: the attacker had to compromise multiple independent security controls to succeed. The incident is a sharp supply-chain warning for the AI tooling ecosystem - LiteLLM sits in the inference path of countless applications, so a poisoned release would have harvested credentials downstream at scale. (read more).

Gary Marcus rounds up evidence that US banks and financial infrastructure are starting to take real hits from AI-enabled attacks. Generative AI, cheap and improving, is a near-perfect tool for attackers: it produces plausible phishing text and deepfaked video at virtually zero cost, and one cybercrew has already bilked a Hong Kong bank out of $25 million. LLMs also remain notoriously susceptible to jailbreaks and prompt-injection attacks, for which no known solution exists. Marcus adds two structural worries: AI-generated code is being deployed by people who don't fully understand it, and autogenerated code has already introduced new security holes in some cases; and the current US administration is deregulating and cutting the federal workforce - including, he warns, the cybersecurity capacity meant to defend exactly this infrastructure. His question: who knows what piece of infrastructure might be attacked next. (read more).

New shapes of model behavior

Fable 5.1 invents and reads its own steganographic codes in images, TypeSafe's mammoth-1 and elephant-1 top the decision-model rankings, and OpenClaw's creator admits to $10M of token spend in a month.

An experiment in machine steganography: since frontier LLMs like Claude Fable 5.1 generate images by writing SVG, researchers asked how such a model would hide a message inside an image it draws - and whether a fresh instance of the same model could decode that message from the rendered image alone, with no SVG source and no pre-agreed scheme. The results show Fable 5.1 inventing working encodings on its own - in one example, a row of stars whose heights spell out a message alphabetically ('The monster acts on the moonshine module'), readable left to right - and successfully decoding its own hidden messages from pixels. A brief comparison with GPT-6 Astra on the same task is included. Beyond the charm, the work probes a real capability question: models that can create and read covert channels in their outputs matter for steganography, watermarking, and hidden-instruction risk. (read more).

TypeSafe released the technical report behind what it calls the world's best decision models: mammoth-1 (27B) and elephant-1 (9B). The decision-model format, which the company introduced in September 2026 with Jev and the System One API - named for Kahneman's fast, intuitive mode of thinking - targets quick, single-shot judgments rather than long chains of reasoning, and the report's refrain is that training a frontier decision model is about data, data, data. On the two community benchmarks, mammoth-1 ranks first of 208 models on the Jev Decision Index (37 public benchmarks plus private tests) and both models place at the top of the Artificial Analysis Decision Index covering knowledge, reasoning, tool calling, and safety. The release continues the industry's split between deliberative reasoning models and fast decision models optimized for latency and cost in agent loops. (read more).

agent-usage-guard is a Claude Code mod that stops the CLI from spending a five-hour usage window in ten minutes. Running inside Claude Code, it reads the plan's live usage and holds runaway spend before it happens - intercepting subagent fan-out, heavy-context churn, and retry loops at the moment an agent starts, a prompt is sent, or a tool call runs, rather than reporting the damage afterward. The guard's limits apply across every Claude Code session on the machine, and caps on fan-out and context apply at any usage level, not just near the limit. It is a community answer to agentic coding's most common complaint: the bill arriving all at once. (read more).
