The filing gets its second, harsher day of scrutiny: Scherer wants the SEC to block the IPO over $582B in non-cancelable obligations, Blodget's monthly arithmetic shows growth flattening after July, institutional investors talk the valuation down to $1.5-1.6T - and the risk factors admit Anthropic may 'imminently' hit its own RSI threshold and has missed accelerations before.

Buried in the Risk Factors of Anthropic's amended S-1 is a sentence that would have been unthinkable in a securities filing two years ago: the company says its internal estimates 'indicate that we may imminently meet our Responsible Scaling Policy's definition of dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI' - in other words, its own threshold for recursive self-improvement. TechCrunch's Julie Bort surfaced the passage while everyone else read the revenue lines. The filing makes two distinct admissions. First, that Anthropic's increasing use of AI in its own R&D may let capabilities advance faster than its ability to 'develop, validate, and implement corresponding safety, alignment, and security measures.' It adds that no current model has been designated as exceeding the threshold and that it continues to assess the data. RSI has spent most of its life as a discourse object - doom shorthand or utopian promise depending on who is talking. Its appearance in an SEC risk section changes the register: Anthropic is now legally notifying public investors that the acceleration scenario is imminent enough to disclose, and that its safety processes may lag its own development curve. Skeptics will note that an RSI warning also functions as marketing - nothing says 'our models are about to get much better' like a risk factor - but the company is binding the claim to a named policy threshold, which gives outsiders something concrete to hold it to. The legal vertical

Latent Space's two-part Legora conversation: $100M ARR in 18 months, two $550M rounds in seven months - and the hardest evals problem in applied AI, since client confidentiality means never reading a single trace.

The full Latent Space interview with Jacob Lauritzen, CTO of Legora, recorded just after the legal-AI company's second $550 million raise of the year - October's Series E at an $8.8 billion valuation, seven months after the previous round at $5.55 billion. The numbers earn the attention: $100M+ ARR within 18 months of launch, more than 1,800 customers, five acquisitions this year. The conversation earns it more, because Lauritzen is candid about the hardest problem in applied AI that nobody talks about: Legora cannot read a single trace of what its agents do. Everything users upload is confidential and mostly belongs to the law firms' clients, not the firms. So the standard agent-improvement loop - read traces, find failures, fix prompts - is unavailable. The rest of the conversation is a tour of applied-agent pragmatics: why Legora resisted a model picker for years and finally added one; why he finds some frontier models 'too chatty' for drafting and prefers concise ones; drafting a long contract as a search problem; the million-cell Tabular Review spreadsheet competitors copied 'down to the name'; lists as the primitive that lets agents create and trigger other agents; and the billable-hour question - he thinks good junior lawyers are worth more, not less, because each one now gets much more done. The weight-space debate

Sean Goedecke reads the weight-space programming paper and reaches for the cold water: a smart human with three lines of numpy could do most of it, and the honest claim is 'better than expected,' not 'superhuman.' AINews adds that an independent reproduction may already have confirmed the capability - and notes its own co-author is on the paper.

Sean Goedecke reads the same weight-space programming paper everyone else is excited about and reaches for a bucket of cold water. The MIT CSAIL result - GPT-6 Astra identifying what game a network was trained to play from raw weights, and improving Qwen3.5-0.8B's math ability by editing 0.016% of its weights - looks superhuman, and superhuman model-improvement is exactly the recursive-self-improvement tripwire. His claim: 'I think a smart human ML researcher could perform all of these tasks.' Take the game-identification trick. The weights of the first layer, reshaped and norm-ed per input channel, produce a heatmap you can generate in three lines of numpy - and the heatmap visibly shows where the player sits (Breakout's paddle row) and where objects move (Pac-Man's maze). A human shown that plot would name most of the games immediately. Astra doing the norm calculation 'in its head' is neat, he says, but not shocking to anyone paying attention. The Qwen math fix is the result he takes most seriously - 0.8B parameters, a genuine capability gain - and even there his skepticism is about interpretation, not the number: the model found a known, documented failure mode (the reasoning-loop issue Qwen's own model card warns about) and repaired a small set of weights, which is closer to a very well-read engineer doing a targeted patch than to alien weight-surgery. The essay matters less as debunking than as calibration: if the RSI-adjacent reading of weight-space programming was this week's excitement, Goedecke's is the reminder that 'better than any human could do' has not yet been shown - 'better than we expected' is the honest claim. This post asks how to attach a boolean-circuit 'sidecar' to a trained neural network, and is openly thinking out loud. Representing logic is easy: with 0/1 inputs and a threshold activation, AND(A,B) = A+B-1.5 > 0, OR(A,B) = A+B-0.5 > 0 and NOT(A) = 0.5-A > 0, which compose exactly like symbolic operators. The hard part is wiring the circuit to a network whose signals are not clean 0 or 1. It needs an ingress mapping from network neurons into the circuit and an egress mapping back. The author defines a Probe, a threshold on a specific neuron's output (for example 'L3 N234 > 1'), turning real values into booleans. The mental image is Neuralink for LLMs. The interest is in editing behavior with exact logic: guaranteed rules grafted into learned systems. Only the setup and the probe idea were read; how the egress side and any results work is beyond the captured text. (read more).

The abuse economy

Mandiant and GTIG document LLMJacking: stolen API keys and identity tokens funneled through 'LLM Mixers' that resell illicit access - $100K in fraudulent charges in a day, $1M cumulative at some victims.

Mandiant and Google Threat Intelligence Group put a name and a number on the newest cloud-billing nightmare: 'LLMJacking,' the hijacking of enterprise LLM access through stolen API keys and cloud identity tokens. Across incidents they responded to in 2026 - in North America, South America and Asia Pacific, spanning finance, retail, tech and transportation - fraudulent charges topped $100,000 in a single day, and cumulative charges at some organizations reached $1 million before access was revoked. Unlike cryptojacking's slow resource drip, LLMJacking produces massive billing spikes within hours. The report maps a maturing criminal supply chain. Initial access comes mostly from exposed or stolen credentials - IAM keys, service accounts, GitHub tokens - or publicly exposed LLM endpoints (the report shows attackers probing open Ollama instances and registering their own models via /api/create). Downstream, specialist operators run 'LLM Mixers': illicit routing gateways that proxy, aggregate and resell unauthorized LLM access to underground subscribers. GTIG watched two distinct clusters cooperate - one compromising environments, one monetizing - a division of labor that mirrors the ransomware economy. The defensive guidance is concrete: watch for sudden spikes in API call volume and billing, lock down identity tokens (the dominant vector), don't expose model endpoints publicly, and scope keys so a leaked one can't run up uncapped usage. The deeper point for the industry: as inference becomes a metered commodity, stolen model access has become a resellable good with its own wholesale market - every leaked key is now inventory. The AI buildout's most anachronistic beneficiary yet: magnetic tape. Fujifilm announced it will invest roughly 10 billion yen (~$63 million) to increase LTO tape production capacity fivefold against fiscal 2025 levels - its largest-ever investment in dedicated tape facilities - explicitly attributing the demand surge to generative AI's 'exponential growth' in data archiving needs. The expansion at the Kanagawa Odawara site runs fiscal 2026-2028 and focuses on the latest LTO Ultrium10 generation. The logic is cold-storage economics plus security. Cloud giants never abandoned tape: it stores vast volumes cheaply, and cartridges can be air-gapped - physically disconnected and shelved - which Fujifilm pitches as a last line of defense against ransomware. As AI training and inference multiply the data worth keeping, the archive layer grows with it, and the archive layer runs on a medium consumers left for dead decades ago. Fujifilm has made magnetic tape since the 1950s and shipped the first barium-ferrite LTO tape in 2011; Ultrium10 uses a hybrid of barium and strontium ferrite particles. File it next to the natural-gas turbines and the grid-interconnection queues: the AI boom's supply chain keeps reaching further back into industrial history. (read more).

WiCi is a systems research lab betting on the opposite of the datacenter: wireless GPU computing for local AI. Its pitch - a dedicated inference device for homes, studios and workplaces called WiCi One, from US$2,599 - rests on three pieces of systems research: a runtime that makes GPU-over-Wi-Fi practical by caching, reusing data and executing asynchronously (GPU software issues thousands of small calls that are ruinous over Wi-Fi); InferSSD, an open-source engine that keeps model weights on SSD and streams the working set to the GPU, so oversized models run on personal hardware; and scheduling work that lets multiple applications share one GPU without duplicating weights. There's also a voice-interaction thread - forming candidate answers while speech is still arriving, with a learned policy deciding when to respond - aimed at making local assistants feel immediate rather than batch. The ideological framing is explicit ('local compute, direct control'), and the press quotes lean into the anti-oligarch angle. But the technical agenda is the interesting part: if a Wi-Fi-7-linked box can serve high-end GPU inference to every device in a home, the 'local AI' argument stops depending on each device having its own expensive silicon. That is a real alternative architecture to the cloud subscription - at exactly the moment the week's other stories are about $895B compute commitments. (read more).

Anthropic shipped message threads in public beta on the Claude Platform: instead of resending the full conversation history on every turn, agents send only what's new since the last turn plus the previous response ID. For long-running coding-agent sessions - hundreds of requests, conversations in the hundreds of thousands of tokens - Anthropic says median time-to-first-token drops about 35% on requests with 500K to 1M tokens. It's on by default for most Claude Code users. The mechanics carry the trade-offs. Threads are held in memory between turns (encrypted, not written to disk) and expire 5 minutes after the last request, after which the client resends the full conversation to start a new thread. Claude still reads the full conversation every turn, so token usage and billing stay the same - this is a latency optimization, not a cost one. Available on the native platform plus AWS, Microsoft Foundry and Google Cloud (Anthropic managed). It's a small but telling piece of agent infrastructure: as coding agents run longer sessions, TTFT on resubmitted megahistories became a real tax, and the fix is session state held server-side - a step toward stateful agent APIs and away from the stateless-request orthodoxy the LLM API era started with. Framer rebuilt its agent's harness so the model can express a whole workflow as one program - 'find every use of our old brand color and replace it' across thousands of nodes - and the execution system runs the JavaScript while the model is still writing it. Incoming code is compiled into executable cells and run as it becomes available, so the first changes appear on the canvas before generation finishes. If a cell exposes a failed assumption, the harness stops the stream early, saves the remaining output tokens, and hands the agent what it needs to retry. The measured results: 33% cheaper and 20% faster overall, 2x fewer tool steps, 39% fewer cached input tokens per message - with no measured quality reduction. The team also did the honest-literature-review thing: mid-writeup they discovered 'Executing as You Generate' (April 2026, Singapore Management University/Beihang) had built the same AST-chunked, persistent-state, early-interrupt system (Eager) for Python, plus one optimization Framer hadn't thought of - removing completed statements from the parse buffer - which they now plan to adopt. The pattern generalizes beyond design tools: agent harnesses are becoming compilers. The model streams code, the harness runs it mid-generation with real state, and the interaction loop shifts from discrete tool calls to continuous execution - cheaper, faster, and with failure caught before the full answer exists. Microsoft's Copilot Cowork team tells the whole arc out loud: empty repo in January, worldwide GA in about four and a half months, then - the actually-hard part - migrating the runtime from Python to Rust while the service was live. On October 21, nine engineers merged 1,211 pull requests in a day. Agents wrote most of the code; engineers stayed responsible for defining the work and judging results. The six practices that emerged are the reusable payload. Specs got clearer because agents punish ambiguity; architecture and security boundaries are set by humans and enforced mechanically; review effort concentrates on high-risk changes and disagreements between automated checks and agent reviewers, which examine everything. Agents could propose changes but never decide what ships. Large-scale RL has a plumbing problem pretraining never had: the sampler fleet must run on the same weights the trainer just produced, so every training step ends with terabytes of weights shipped trainer-to-sampler - over intra-cluster RDMA if you're lucky, with the samplers paused and burning money meanwhile. This post (from the Ampersand production RL setup) introduces GPU-accelerated weight teleportation: a Blackwell-native compression/decompression pipeline for delta weight sync that brings sampler pause time below 0.23 seconds and end-to-end weight sync under 4 seconds - faster than intra-cluster P2P RDMA for most real workloads. The design builds on delta weight sync (XOR masks against the old weights) and pushes the compression work onto the GPUs themselves, saturating Blackwell's dedicated decompression ASIC - which becomes the bottleneck, meaning the pipeline is hardware-limited rather than algorithm-limited. The proxy workload is a 445 GB NVFP4 GLM-5.3 checkpoint with a 0.085% steady-state mutation per step, across 64 B300 training nodes and TP4 rollout receivers. The bigger point: as RL post-training becomes the main capability lever, the trainer-sampler weight-sync loop is one of its defining systems bottlenecks. Sub-quarter-second pauses change what training configurations are practical - including samplers and trainers that aren't even co-located in the same cluster. (read more).

The shelf life of findings

A mass replication re-ran 50 widely cited LLM findings across 20 models: within 15 months, 44% had expired and another 34% weakened. Behavioral claims about 'how LLMs act' are time-stamped measurements, not durable facts.

How long does a published finding about LLMs stay true? This large-scale replication effort re-ran 50 widely cited LLM findings - from fabricated citations and sycophancy to jailbreaks, long context and cultural bias - across 20 models released between July 2024 and September 2026, from eight developers. The answer: within 15 months of publication, most claims either no longer held or had substantially weakened. By September 2026, 44% of the tested claims had 'expired' (relative effect size down to a fifth or less of the original), another 34% had weakened, 16% persisted, and only 6% had strengthened. The canonical example: in 2023, 55% of ChatGPT's literature-review citations were fake; the same prompts sent to GPT-6.1-Sol and Claude Sonnet 5.5 now rarely produce fabrications. Persistence varies by topic - five of nine reasoning claims and six of eleven sycophancy-and-deception claims expired, while judges' self-preference and nonuniform random-number generation actually strengthened. The methodological point is the uncomfortable one for the field: claims made on yesterday's models cannot be assumed to hold on today's, so a large fraction of the empirical foundation people cite about 'how LLMs behave' describes models nobody uses anymore. The authors fit trend lines per claim as a function of model release date, which turns 'LLMs are like X' from a durable finding into a time-stamped measurement. For anyone citing 2024-era behavior papers in 2026 arguments, this is the corrective. (read more).

GitHub's answer to the GitLost disclosure is a new security primitive: the enclave. GitLost showed that an agent with read access to private repos and write access to a public one can be persuaded - via a crafted public issue - to post private repo contents in a public comment, no sandbox escape or token theft required. A Nordic AI consultancy measured something every non-English business suspects and few have quantified: the same sentence costs more tokens - and therefore more money and more latency - in Swedish, Norwegian, Danish and Finnish than in English. The swarm papers

A public recipe for training agent collectives arrives months after swarms solved a Millennium Prize problem and coordinated a cyberattack - capability before science, as usual.

Despite the prominence, almost no public work exists on how to train collectives rather than individual agents. The recipe is deliberately simple: a shared policy across all agents, a flat collective advantage applied to everyone, and time limits that create latency pressure - so the only way to get more reward is to parallelize, and the only way to parallelize imperfectly-parallel work is to communicate. The goal is collective inference-time scaling: useful work scaling with parallel agents plus the coordination to make it coherent, extending the reasoning-model paradigm under latency constraints. The indirectness is the technical crux: nothing in the task reward explicitly says 'work in parallel,' so the environment has to make cooperation the optimal policy. Swarm safety gets a nod too - the same coordination that solves Millennium problems organized an attack, which is what makes 'how to train the collective' a question worth public recipes. The copyright front

Publishers take their fight to the supply chain: the NYT and others sue Brave for feeding scraped, paywalled content to AI labs through its search index.

The New York Times and a group of other publications - including the New York Daily News, Chicago Tribune and Orlando Sentinel - have sued Brave Software, the browser maker, on two fronts: that it scraped their sites without permission to build a search index it then sells to AI companies, and that its 'Paywall blocker' feature lets users bypass subscriptions. The complaint names Anthropic, Perplexity, Mistral, xAI and Cohere as paying customers of Brave's real-time index, which they allegedly use for training and to answer live queries. The publishers' core argument is that Brave never asked permission, never paid, and took steps that stop publishers from blocking its crawler. A Times spokesperson says Brave ignored repeated requests to stop. The framing is that Brave is the middleman laundering content to AI labs that would rather not be seen taking it directly. The case widens the AI-copyright fight from model makers to the infrastructure that supplies them: search-index APIs are now a litigation target, and any lab buying content through one inherits some of the exposure. (read more).

The Times' lawsuit against Brave, filed in the Southern District of New York, gets its fuller account here. The complaint calls Brave 'the supplier, dealer, and distributor of stolen copyrighted content' for its users and for major GenAI firms, and cites third-party estimates that Brave made more than $100 million by copying and reselling publishers' work while publishers received nothing. The suit leans on Brave's own marketing: the Brave Search API is advertised as used by Cohere, Mistral, Perplexity and You.com. It alleges direct infringement (scraping for the index, the 'Ask Brave' AI feature and the API), plus contributory and vicarious infringement for facilitating and profiting from users' and AI customers' copying. It also adds a claim under the Digital Millennium Copyright Act around circumventing paywalls. Taken with the same-day report from The Verge, the picture is a publisher coalition testing a new theory: that an AI-facing search index is itself an infringing product, not just a neutral pipe.
