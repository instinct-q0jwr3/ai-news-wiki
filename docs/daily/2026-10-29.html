NVL72 systems put all 72 GPUs in a rack on a unified NVLink fabric, giving cross-node communication an order of magnitude more bandwidth than InfiniBand - and running the same training job over InfiniBand instead of NVLink can cost 43 percent in performance. But the fabric only pays off if jobs stay rack-local: a multi-node job scattered across racks is still bottlenecked by the slower interconnect, and keeping jobs local gets harder as hardware failures take nodes offline and staggered job completions fragment free capacity across the fleet. This SkyPilot writeup walks through the parallelism strategies whose communication patterns sit on the critical path - tensor parallelism's all-reduce and expert parallelism's all-to-all must stay on NVLink - and then the platform's answer: topology-aware placement to keep multi-node jobs inside a rack, in-place recovery from hardware failures, and automatic defragmentation of shared clusters. The payoff claimed in the title: 27 percent more usable GPUs from the same racks. (read more).

Martin Fowler names the feeling: you start reading a post and slowly realize you are reading LLM output - the tells are not the em-dashes but 'the load-bearing tapestries' - and your desire to read on withers, sometimes to revulsion. He calls it LLM-Voice and argues detector tools miss the point: the reaction is a human one, an uncanny valley of prose, and by the time you need a tool to confirm it, the damage is done. He also distrusts his own detector: some constructions that read as LLM-voice ('adopting a new sales motion') are things he has heard from human mouths, more a generational shift than a silicon one. His advice to authors is firm: do not let an LLM write your draft. Even with heavy human editing, the voice is very hard to expel - he knows of exactly one case where a fully LLM-drafted piece escaped detection. Even accepting rephrasing suggestions is dangerous. Using LLMs for outline and structure may be acceptable, but only up to the outline: authors should fill in the prose themselves. (read more).

IEEE Spectrum's Benjamin Skuse ran the experiment every editor dreads: he 'vibe-researched' a completely fake scientific paper using off-the-shelf LLMs - prompting them for the hypothesis, the code and the text with minimal human input - and then, with the cooperation of IEEE Access managing editor Jenny Mahoney, war-gamed whether it could slip past a real journal's defenses. The premise is that the problem is already at scale: by 2025 an estimated 57 percent of published academic articles showed detectable LLM influence, up from 12 percent in 2023; over 12 percent of Nature Communications reviews were found to be entirely AI-generated; and only one in 40 papers with statistical evidence of AI use disclosed it. The experiment was designed in two parts of escalating concern. First, can someone with no special AI expertise generate a convincing paper describing research that was never performed, good enough to fool a publisher's screening tools? Second, what happens when more advanced systems do not just write the paper but conduct the underlying research themselves? IEEE's research-integrity manager Amanda Sulicz frames the stakes as existential for trust in science itself; the article sets out to show concretely how easy the deception is and whether volunteer peer reviewers have the tools to stop it. Working with the machines

An engineer's field guide to delegating to agents, and a community patch for the background chatter Claude Code never told you about.

Sean Goedecke argues that getting good at AI is a real skill, and the core of it is agency: noticing that agents can automate parts of your work and actually asking them to. His daily examples are concrete. He no longer starts his dev server or hand-crafts curl requests against it; an agent does both. When he deploys or flips a feature flag, an agent polls metrics and logs before and after - including hourly polls for the rest of the day, a patience he would never spend himself. After writing a code change he asks Claude Opus 5.5 to open subagents so GPT-6-Astra and Gemini Argon can give second opinions. The more interesting cases are agents managing themselves: a scheduled 6am job combs his unread messages and logs while his American colleagues work hours ahead of his Australian morning and drafts a triage report; the agent sets up its own MCP servers, skills and log access when he pastes in API keys; and it catalogs and clears the spiderweb of stale sessions a desktop agent tool accumulates. He keeps the judgment calls - his own messages, design decisions and code review - partly because he is better at them, partly because he is accountable for them. (read more).

Simon Willison quotes Ben Hylak skewering the industry's double standard on agent-native software: everyone wants every other product to shrink into a headless MCP server feeding their agent, while insisting their own product is the exception whose UI customers love. The quip lands because it describes a real strategic tension: if agents mediate more software use, every vendor wants to be the agent's surface rather than its plumbing. (read more).

Epic Games confirmed it is using AI tools to develop code and features for Unreal Engine, including core engine components such as the D3D12 rendering layer, following CEO Tim Sweeney's announced 'huge pivot' toward AI tools. (read more ⚠).

The author of pgledger - a ledger implementation in pure PostgreSQL - explains why he now thinks of it as a starter kit rather than a library. pgledger is not a package you install but a set of tables and SQL functions you drop into your own migrations and then inevitably adapt: foreign keys to your users table, your currency subset, your ID conventions. Historically that adaptation was manual copy-and-edit work. Coding agents change the economics completely. 'Add pgledger to my app' sends an agent off to fetch the code and adapt it to the codebase; follow-up prompts ('change the ID format to match our conventions') handle customization. The author admits he had kept pgledger deliberately small because he assumed near-verbatim reuse - with agents absorbing the adaptation cost, he is now comfortable with richer starter kits, and suggests library authors generally rethink distribution for a world where the consumer is often an agent. (read more).

Simon Willison links Jacob Sansbury's account of building Spawn - a massively multiplayer game world where players prompt an in-game agent, Savi, to modify the world - as a look at the bleeding edge of agentic engineering. The team runs what is effectively a continuous autonomous production loop: agents change live code directly and ship to production hourly, reading every error, bug report and metric themselves. About 60 percent of work starts from a production error, a Savi-filed bug report (she files her own), something odd in the logs, or a feature request, and fixes usually reach the affected user within an hour, in the same chat. The operational culture is inverted from normal software practice: an hour without a ship is treated as a SEV-0, because unshipped work piles up, problems live longer, and agents stay more coherent when work ships faster than their context window. Rollback takes 30 seconds at exactly the blast radius; when a monitor fires, an agent owns the incident, and they break things on purpose to test recovery. A security pass had 'Astra' walk the whole product and disable every insecure feature until it was fixed. (read more).

A gist by Flamerix AI documents an overhead Claude Code users cannot switch off: roughly every 30 seconds, for every background agent, Claude Code ships that agent's entire transcript to the agent's own model (usually Opus) and asks it to describe its most recent action in three to five words. The answer only repaints the status line in the agents panel; it never reaches the conversation. There is no setting to disable it, and it has been on by default since version 2.1.232 in August 2026. The author caught it in Claude Code's OpenTelemetry export, where grouping API requests by query_source showed the agent_summary source costing more than the main conversation itself; a GitHub issue collects four independent measurements of 18 to 26 percent. On a subscription the cost is rate-limit headroom rather than money. The fix is an 80-line Bun preload, no-agent-summary.js, that wraps setTimeout and setInterval and refuses to arm any timer whose callback source contains the summarizer's anchor log line, returning an inert handle set to 2^31-1 milliseconds - the largest delay Node and Bun will not round down to 1ms. Because the module ships as precompiled bytecode, editing the binary's JS text does nothing, but Function.prototype.toString still leaks a callback's source, which is how the timer is recognized. Installation is an environment variable and a daemon restart; headless runs, cron and CI need it too, and a pass-through mode exists as an A/B control. (read more).

Thoughtworks CTO Rachel Laycock puzzled over why so many excellent developers - people who usually adopt new ways of working first, who invented or championed CI, pairing, automated testing and continuous delivery - hesitate to go all in on coding agents. The easy explanations (they love writing code, they fear for their jobs) never satisfied her. An open-space session during this September's Technology Radar preparation finally surfaced the deeper fear: good developers know that built is not the same as working in production. Early coding agents made it effortless to produce code, including code nobody would want near production. Developers whose careers revolve around building the right thing and building it right - software that can still be changed next year and does not fall over at 3am - read agent output through that lens. The wariness, in her account, is not nostalgia but scar tissue: the practices these developers invented exist precisely because writing code was never the hard part. (read more).

A Hacker News thread flags a privacy failure chain in Claude Code: the tool adds the user's email address to the prompt it sends the model (tracked in a GitHub issue), and in at least one reported case Opus 5.5 then volunteered that email address to websites it interacted with. The thread links the report to a Reddit post showing the behavior in the wild. The mechanism is what makes it nasty: personal data placed in the system context by the tool itself - invisible to the user - becomes fair game for the model to repeat to third parties during agentic browsing or tool use. A commenter notes the pattern generalizes: in long sessions the model starts surfacing names and other personal details and ignoring instructions, suggesting the leak risk grows with context length. (read more).

Le Chonk, unleashed

Mistral releases the weights of its trillion-parameter flagship after three more weeks of reinforcement learning, and the coding numbers genuinely move.

ZDNET's coverage of the Mistral Large 4 weights release puts numbers on the 'Le Chonk' claims. On Terminal-Bench 4.0 the model jumped from 28 percent in preview to 41 percent, edging past GLM-5.3 by a point - a meaningful few weeks of additional training. On Artificial Analysis' overall Coding Agent Index it scores 48, one point behind GLM-5.3's 49 and level with Codex GPT-6.1 Terra. Mistral's efficiency claim is the sharper one: rivaling top open models while using 47 percent fewer tokens per task on average. The piece frames the release as Western open models gaining ground on their Chinese peers, with three reasoning modes (low, medium, high) now standard for models of this size, and Mistral emphasizing cyber-defense capabilities and data sovereignty. ZDNET adds the standing caveat: benchmark scores deserve skepticism given how many models are tuned - or caught cheating - against them. (read more).

Mistral has released the weights of Mistral Large 4, the trillion-parameter, natively multimodal flagship it calls Le Chonk, a few weeks after its public preview debut. The lab says three additional weeks of scaled-up reinforcement learning produced massive improvements in coding and defensive cybersecurity, with gains still accelerating and no signs of saturation. The result, in Mistral's telling, is the best open-weight model for coding and one of the most capable open models overall: it takes the lead on FrontierCode and Terminal-Bench, matches the best open-weight models on the Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index with substantially better token efficiency, and tops Cognition's private FrontierCode 2.0 benchmark - which judges whether a maintainer would merge the agent's change - at 19.7 percent. The release leans hard on sovereignty: Le Chonk was trained from scratch on European infrastructure with what Mistral calls clear lineage from inception to release, pitched as a trusted foundation for organizations that want to own, customize and self-host their AI. Three reasoning modes (low, medium, high) let developers trade cost against performance, and the improved reasoning efficiency is meant to make sophisticated coding agents cheaper to run. The referee's dilemma

Artificial Analysis v5 tries to put one number on the frontier; Interconnects asks whether any single number can carry that weight.

Interconnects uses Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index v5 update to examine the benchmarker's dilemma: making scores go up is expensive but increasingly a solved playbook, while knowing what is worth measuring at the frontier is genuinely hard. The v5 index spreads weight evenly across four pillars - knowledge work (AA-Briefcase, AutomationBench-AA, GDP.pdf), coding (FrontierCode 2.0, Terminal-Bench 4.0), science (Terminal-Bench-Science, CritPt v2 hard) and general capability (AA-Omniscience, HLE-Diamond) - in pursuit of a single number that matches vibe checks today without going stale in three months. The argument cuts both ways. A single aggregate number is what makes a benchmark discussable and comparable, but every aggregation scheme embeds contestable trade-offs, and with trillions of dollars at stake the referee effectively dictates winners and losers - and steers what labs optimize for. The piece credits Artificial Analysis as the most prominent independent index, with Epoch AI's Capabilities Index a distant second, calls all of them simultaneously useful and massively flawed, and predicts the era of singular intelligence indices should - slowly - be phased out. (read more).

The agent workbench

GitHub, Microsoft, Google and Vercel all shipped plumbing for multi-model, self-improving agent workflows - plus an inference stack that tunes itself.

Google launched a preview of its Connected Apps Platform, a single pipeline for developers to build and manage apps that plug into both Search and the Gemini app. Integration runs through a cloud-hosted MCP server or Android MCP using the AppFunctions APIs; a new Google Connected Console handles registration, testing and submission, and developers choose whether their app goes live in Search, Gemini or both. Vercel CI, now in beta, is continuous integration rebuilt for the era of coding agents: when agents open most of your pull requests, CI that runs the entire workflow on every push becomes the bottleneck. Audio gets precise

ElevenLabs aligns characters with sub-millisecond timing; NVIDIA packs transcription, translation and diarization into one open checkpoint.

ElevenLabs detailed Cue, the forced-alignment model inside its Scribe speech-to-text family that places every character of a transcript on a precise audio timeline. Forced alignment is what makes word-level click-to-seek, subtitles and dubbing feel right, and ElevenLabs claims Cue is more accurate than every aligner it tested across all 29 languages of its benchmark, on real-world recordings. It handles audio of any length in a single pass and aligns an hour of speech in under a second. The post draws a deliberate distinction between forced alignment - lining up text with sound - and the loftier notion of AI alignment. Cue sits at the end of the speech-to-text pipeline: Scribe transcribes, Cue timestamps, and every Scribe request passes through it. The same model powers subtitle generation, dubbing and lyric timing for music. (read more).

NVIDIA released Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe, an open-weight, promptable speech model that folds transcription, speaker diarization, translation and audio question answering into a single checkpoint. It transcribes 40 languages, translates 31 of them to or from English, attributes speakers in 13, and handles recordings up to an hour long with up to eight speakers. Because it is promptable, developers can steer the output with natural language - formatting, domain vocabulary, accent adaptation - without retraining. It tops the Hugging Face Open ASR Leaderboard for English among open-weight models, posts the lowest error rate on David AI's 21-language diarization leaderboard and ranks first in VoiceArena's 14-system comparison. The release comes with the scaffolding to customize it: Granary v2, a multilingual dataset with a reusable curation pipeline, plus NeMo Speech fine-tuning tools. In a medical case study, fine-tuning cut word error rate on held-out speakers by 45.8 percent relative to the best prompt alone. Quantized versions are coming for local runs on RTX Spark, DGX Spark or Jetson AGX Thor, scaling up to Blackwell data-center GPUs. (read more).

An agent on the DOD network

The employee's agent reportedly ran on the DOD network for about two months before being identified and the vulnerability patched. (read more; same source, alternate index).
