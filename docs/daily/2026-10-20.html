The solo operator's contingency plan

A self-hosted dead man's switch that tells the right people in the right order.

Death Plan Switch is a self-hosted dead man's switch built by a solo web-studio owner in regional New South Wales, born from a real fragility: his clients' sites, domains and hosting all run through him, and if he died nobody would know where anything was. His plan: everything the business runs on lives in one encrypted document, his backup developer holds the password, his lawyer a second copy - and this tool is the missing middle that notices he has gone quiet and tells the right people in the right order. It emails a weekly check-in; if he stops responding, a staged escalation runs - first a next-of-kin confirmation, then client notifications, at most 13 days after the last check-in. Design details show care: opening the email never counts (scanners open links), only pressing the button does, and a false alarm can be cancelled at any stage, with everyone already contacted told it was a mistake. What benchmarks and citations are worth

Goodhart's Law comes for the leaderboards, and 50,000 fake statistics come for the chatbots.

Contrary Research argues that AI benchmarks have fallen to Goodhart's Law: now that scores drive rankings, sales, and even bets, frontier labs explicitly hill-climb them through reinforcement learning, so recent gains reflect targeting rather than broad capability. The report's clearest case is Arena, the former Chatbot Arena, which drew over a million monthly visitors by April 2025 and became a fixture in model launches: Meta's Llama 4 Maverick posted an experimental-chat Elo of 1417, second on the leaderboard ahead of GPT-4.5, Grok 3, and DeepSeek R1, only for users to find the ranking did not survive contact with reality. With Big Tech planning more than $700 billion of 2026 capex on models sold by these numbers, the report examines why flawed benchmarks actively steer training in wrong directions, why good evaluation is expensive to build, and what a new generation of benchmarks could look like. (read more).

A GEO researcher at Peec AI describes seeding a throwaway domain, stateglobe.com, with 50,000 entirely fabricated statistics pages generated in a single day with GPT-4.1, later adding 15,000 listicles, for a total cost of about $50. Seven months on, Google has deindexed the site, but ChatGPT has not: the fake numbers have driven more than 10,000 AI-referred sessions, attracted over 330 referring domains including Shopify, Bluehost, and DHL, and surfaced in an academic paper and a bank's investment research. His argument is that LLMs producing time-sensitive content hunt for reliable-looking data to fill knowledge gaps, and statistics pages are exactly what they reach for. Where most GEO advice targets the moment of the AI answer, this experiment maps what happens after, when people reuse the output in presentations, shopping lists, and their own sites. (read more).

Beyond the LLM shape

Cloudflare ships a token-free extraction model and Exa retools search for agents.

Cloudflare announces a preview of Clairic, billed as the first System One extraction model, hosted on Workers AI as @cf/cloudflare/clairic with open weights promised soon. Where the decision models that defined the System One category (TypeSafe's Jev, Cloudflare's Clef) return probabilities over a listed set of options, Clairic extracts, computes, estimates, and forecasts typed values directly: numbers with an 80% range, plus names, dates, and lists. Because it answers in a single pass without generating tokens, it cannot mistype an answer or hallucinate in the LLM sense, returns null when the input contains no answer, and attaches confidence scores; Cloudflare claims higher accuracy, faster latency, and lower cost than routing the same structured-extraction calls through frontier LLMs. (read more).

Agents learn to do less

Meta's thin-orchestrator pattern, a long-running-work workspace, and Gemini Notebook's co-creation flow.

Meta engineers describe the thin orchestrator pattern that emerged from automating data-column onboarding across three repositories, three languages, and up to six pipeline stages. Their first single agent, loaded with every schema, rule, and pattern, worked in demos but hallucinated in production, cross-contaminating concepts between subsystems. The fix was architectural: a 1,300-line orchestrator that routes 20+ workflows to 14 specialized subagents and does zero domain work itself, running a fixed seven-step protocol in which only one step involves LLM reasoning, isolated inside a subagent with only its domain knowledge. Deterministic workflow templates sequence the phases, so new use cases need new templates rather than new agents. They cite a Mount Sinai preprint in which orchestrated multiagent systems held 65.3% accuracy on 80-task batches where single agents fell to 16.6%, with up to 65-fold token reduction; their own hand-prompted single-agent alternative hit 70-80% only with per-task prompt engineering and collapsed to 10-20% without it. A critical enterprise security flaw dissected

Horizon3 walks through an unauthenticated path-traversal RCE in FortiSIEM's gRPC service.

Horizon3 dissects CVE-2026-106173, a critical FortiSIEM vulnerability Fortinet disclosed on 13 October 2026 that allows unauthenticated arbitrary file write leading to remote code execution. The bug lives in a gRPC service the team had not examined before, phQueryMaster on tcp/27918, which binds every interface and does not request client certificates, so any remote client can connect unauthenticated. Its SendQueryResult RPC builds a file path by concatenating attacker-controlled query-id and src-ip values without validation: a query-id like ../../../../../tmp/x traverses outside the query directory, and the streamed bytes become the file's contents, giving arbitrary directory and partial filename control. (read more).

Measuring models and the bots they fight

New SlopCodeBench rankings and a look at how bot detection holds up against modern agents.

Humanlayer's October 2026 SlopCodeBench writeup expands the benchmark to 36 problems and 196 checkpoints per setting. The headline metric is strict pass rate (every test case passed while keeping regression tests green): frontier models land in a 12.2-16.3% band, with gpt-6 astra highest at 16.3%, versus 14.8% for gpt-5.5 back in May; the looser core pass rate sits at 65.8-74.0%. A notable finding: medium effort beats XHigh on some newer models, and Opus 5.5 did better on medium than on XHigh, even though XHigh costs 2.11-4.22x as much; where Opus 5.5 Medium had more strict passes, the XHigh runs wrote roughly 5x cleaner code. A qualitative Part II on slop patterns is promised. (read more).

Researchers revisit their bot-detection benchmark with modern adversaries: conventional scripted browsers, stealth browsers that hide automation signals, and AI agents that navigate pages autonomously. A year ago the five leading systems detected 33-87% of automated browsers; now, across Proof of Human, Google reCAPTCHA v3, hCaptcha, Cloudflare Turnstile, and Cloudflare Bot Management, detection ranges from 9% to 99%. The test used a five-question survey mixing multiple-choice and open responses, with each of five adversaries attempting it 20 times per system, yielding 89-100 evaluable sessions each. Proof of Human led at 99% aggregated detection; behavioral signals alongside device signals again outperformed device-only approaches. (read more).

Community tooling for agents

Anthropic reimagines source control, plus Show HN launches: an OSINT grapher, a Rust gateway, a math grader, a traffic router.

VoidAccess is a self-hostable, open-source OSINT pipeline that turns dark-web research queries into structured threat intelligence: collection, enrichment, relationship mapping, and export in one workflow, aimed at security researchers, threat-intel teams, and authorized investigators. It installs from pip, runs investigations from the CLI, maintains persistent actor profiles with timelines and analyst notes, and exports to STIX, MISP, Sigma, YARA, Snort, Suricata, CSV, Markdown, or JSON. LLM analysis is pluggable across OpenRouter, Groq, OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini, and local Ollama models, and the tool degrades gracefully: sources without API keys skip cleanly, and without PyTorch it falls back to a deterministic SHA-256 encoder instead of vector embeddings. (read more).

Penmentor turns a pen-tablet math session into a mentored one: while working by hand in xournal++, pressing a key has Claude, Gemini, or GPT mark up the page like a teacher with coloured pens, circling mistakes, ticking what's right, giving hints instead of answers, and rendering a 3Blue1Brown-style manim animation of the idea. It runs on the AI command-line agent the user already has logged in, so it rides an existing subscription rather than a new API bill, with the model picked in Settings. Linux-only (X11, best with i3), needing rofi, slop, xdotool, and PyQt6; MIT licensed. (read more).

Respan (YC W24) launches an open-source AI gateway in Rust where everything is a plugin: providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini), protocols (chat, realtime voice, MCP), modalities, and gateway features like fallbacks, PII redaction, and smart routing. The origin story is LiteLLM fatigue: after building a gateway on LiteLLM since 2024 that serves thousands of orgs, their wrapper code exceeded 100,000 lines, and a routine upgrade in January caused intermittent connection errors they never diagnosed, leaving them backporting fixes by hand. Inspired by Nginx, the redesign keeps a tiny core that only dispatches events, so teams assemble their own AI infrastructure without forking the gateway. (read more).

Rouler is split tunnelling for Windows: rules that send each app, IP, or subnet out the network interface you choose, so a privacy VPN, a work tunnel, and the normal connection run side by side. It is not a VPN or a firewall; it works with any interface Windows exposes, including the official WireGuard, Tailscale, Mullvad, OpenVPN, and AmneziaWG clients, via its own small Windows network driver written in Rust. A live activity view shows every connection, the rule it matched, and whether it was diverted, routed by Windows, or blocked, and rules can be created by right-clicking any connection. The author built it out of hatred for background software that interferes with everything, promising an interface that stays out of the way; rules for packaged apps and folders are coming. (read more).

Anthropic explains why it built Scion, an in-house version control system, migrating its monorepo off git in five months: coding is no longer the bottleneck, developer infrastructure built for human scale is. Merged changes went from about 2,500 per day in May 2026 to over 30,000 per day by September, and the company expects demand for merges to keep growing as each bottleneck is removed. Alongside Scion it shipped a code review surface (Antique), code search, source-control IAM, and new machinery for landing large volumes of reviewed code. An essay on what AI writing costs the reader. The author, whose bachelor thesis showed even simple word-statistics can identify an author, argues AI averages style away, so the person behind the text disappears and with them the context that helps a reader interpret it. It also loses precision: to stitch text together, models make assumptions and choose ambiguous wording, which the writer, holding a clear idea in mind, cannot easily spot. Writing is also how we challenge our own ideas, and skipping that step removes the chance to catch inconsistencies. His measurable claim: he reads AI-generated text at least a third slower, and model verbosity multiplies total reading time, quickly evaporating whatever time the writer saved once more than a few readers are involved.
