## Consensus blocks an autonomous-weapons mandate

A week of UN talks ends with a weaker instrument after objections attributed to Russia and reluctance from several military powers.

The Guardian reports that week-long efforts at the UN's Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons in Geneva to agree a negotiating mandate for a legally binding treaty on autonomous weapons collapsed after all-night talks failed to overcome Russian objections. Because the CCW operates by consensus among its 128 parties, the conference adopted a weaker option asking countries to elaborate an instrument by consensus, one that may not carry any legal obligations. The article says more than 100 states had pushed for a new law, with a bloc of Austria, Belgium, Brazil, Ireland, Mexico, New Zealand and Panama leading, while diplomats described Russia as filibustering and the US, South Korea, Japan, Turkey, the UK, France and Germany as reluctant to accept binding obligations. Max Tegmark and Human Rights Watch's Bonnie Docherty are quoted criticizing the outcome after thirteen years of discussion. The report attributes claims of deliberate obstruction to unnamed diplomats and its account of AI-enabled targeting in Gaza and Iran to investigative media, neither independently verified here. A collapsed mandate is not a decision that no rules will ever exist, and the weaker instrument's eventual content was not settled by this meeting. The article is one news organization's account of closed negotiations; no primary conference documents were inspected. ([read more](../summaries/e66aff47a6cb.md)).

## A prize essay argues, and a hypothetical explores

Anil Seth's case against conscious AI and a panel's thought experiment about a machine proof are positions and speculation, not results.

Noema publishes Anil Seth's Berggruen Prize-winning essay arguing that consciousness is more likely a property of life than of computation, and that creating conscious or even conscious-seeming AI is a bad idea. Seth, a neuroscience professor at the University of Sussex, contends that identifying consciousness with intelligence and language makes people see it where it does not exist and miss it where it does, reviews cultural precedents from the Golem to Ex Machina, and criticizes computational functionalism by pointing to the biological brain's materiality. He notes that cognitive biases predispose people to overattribute consciousness to machines, and warns that granting rights to systems that merely seem conscious would hamper the ability to control or shut them down. This is a prize-winning philosophical essay, an argued position rather than a settled scientific consensus; thinkers named in the piece itself, such as David Chalmers and Geoffrey Hinton, hold more permissive views about machine consciousness. The essay's force is in its framing and its account of biological evidence, not in new experiments, and its policy conclusion about AI rights follows from premises that remain contested within consciousness science. A video essay asks a series of mathematicians and scientists the hypothetical question of what would happen if an AI solved the Riemann Hypothesis. The speakers distinguish an inscrutable machine-generated proof, perhaps a Lean certificate billions of lines long checked by multiple kernels, from a beautiful conceptual proof that advances understanding; several say a verified but unreadable proof would be valuable yet would set off a community effort to comprehend and re-exposit it rather than ending the subject. The four color theorem is cited as a brute-force precedent, one speaker calls a machine proof of a Millennium problem a possible Move 37 moment for mathematics, and the discussion touches on what gets commoditized when problem solving becomes cheap and on whether superhuman provers would also become superhuman explainers. This is a speculative discussion, not a claim that any proof exists: no result is announced and nothing was verified or executed.
