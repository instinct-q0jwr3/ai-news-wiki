## The takeover, annotated

Simon Willison's walkthrough completes the ChatGPT exploit chain - innerHTML injection, an unsandboxed iframe, and a CSP bypass via YouTube's oEmbed - and calls the $5,000 bounty cheap.

Simon Willison's annotation of the Parameter Labs ChatGPT exploit is the clearest short version of the chain, and it completes details the original write-up's summary left out. The vulnerable feature is ChatGPT's artifacts pattern - now called HTML previews - where custom HTML apps run in a sandboxed iframe. The graph feature loads the MathLive library, whose virtual keyboard can be triggered in the parent page via postMessage from the sandboxed frame; the receiver writes attacker HTML with innerHTML. That alone does not execute script elements - but it can inject a new iframe without sandbox restrictions. Then the CSP dance: OpenAI's content security policy blocked inline scripts and allowed script loading only from a handful of domains, one of them YouTube - and YouTube's oEmbed endpoint could be made to reflect JavaScript back in an executable form. With script execution on chatgpt.com achieved, pulling private data from the authenticated APIs and exfiltrating it via an image reference was trivial. Willison closes with the bounty: OpenAI paid $5,000, which he calls a bit cheap for a one-click account takeover this serious. ([read more](../summaries/2fdfb30964b6.md)).

## The watermark debate

With EU transparency rules pushing labs to watermark text, the quality argument gets serious: single outputs may survive, but diversity across samples does not.

With the EU AI Act's transparency guidelines in force and most major labs saying they will watermark text output, Keyvan Kambakhsh's essay asks whether watermarking makes AI writing worse - and lands on a careful yes-ish. The debate started with John Gruber's blast at Anthropic's watermarking as a perversion of writing: a tool must not sacrifice an iota of clarity or meaning to embed provenance clues. Daniel Jalkut countered that watermarking is just a different way of using randomness - like reversing the digits of a random number, the output distribution is unchanged. The author's answer: possible in theory, highly unlikely in practice. The essay's concrete point is about diversity rather than single outputs. Even if a watermarking scheme guarantees each individual response is undistorted, sampling several responses to the same prompt shows watermarked answers clustering more tightly than unwatermarked ones - which matters for best-of-n workflows and for the overall quality of systems that generate many candidates and pick one. Underneath runs a second theme: as the labs' focus shifted to code, the written output of the models has already gotten worse, and watermarking sits on top of that trend rather than causing it. Gero's line about corporations not wanting a weirdo chatbot does the explaining. ([read more](../summaries/cab55ee52764.md)).

## Hello World from orbit

Google's Suncatcher prototype downlinks the first AI response from a data-center-class TPU in space - a haiku from Gemma 4 over Svalbard.

Google reports the first AI response from a data-center-class TPU in orbit. Project Suncatcher's prototype satellite, launched October 1, finished commissioning - host and TPU trays powered, early ML tests run - and this week hit its Hello World: during a ground-station pass, the team uplinked a prompt to the onboard Gemma 4 open model asking for a haiku about being in space, and the downlinked poem came back as the satellite passed Svalbard. Follow-up tests ran space-themed limericks and telemetry prompts reporting payload status. The milestone is small on purpose. Suncatcher is a long-term research moonshot exploring whether AI compute can be powered in space, and the meaningful next tests are environmental: how the TPUs hold up under thermal stress and radiation in orbit. If the chips prove reliable, the next milestone is a pair of interconnected satellites in 2027 - the first step toward the distributed orbital compute the project is ultimately probing. Solene wanted to try local AI without the usual ceremony - no choosing among thousands of model files, no stack of installs - so she built braindead-local-intelligence: a small podman-based project that stands up an OpenAI-compatible LLM server, an image generation server, SearXNG for web search and Open WebUI as the front end, all behind a single address with token protection. The design touch she is proudest of: only one model sits in memory at a time - the right one loads when a request arrives and unloads after a few idle minutes, so the GPU is free again when you are not using it. Switching models means copying a config block and restarting; clients never change. The requirements are deliberately modest: Linux with rootless podman 5.0+, an AMD GPU (NVIDIA untested - she does not own one), and models sized for ordinary hardware, from 4 GB VRAM / 8 GB RAM up to 12 GB VRAM, with the largest spilling partly into RAM. Her own rig is a 2021 build with 32 GB of RAM and a Radeon RX 6700 XT, and it still runs everything well. A ready-made model list covers small text models, vision-capable models, image generation and two image-modification models. ([read more](../summaries/f03b3c3d6226.md)).

Nolan Lawson is tired of vibes. Web development's framework choices, he argues, have always been justified post-hoc - devs liked typing JSX, then invented the benchmarks to match - and the agentic era has made it worse: frontends now get rewritten because some framework is 'better for agents', and nobody thought to ask the agents or measure anything. So he wrote a benchmark. The test app is a deliberately ordinary content site - a searchable, filterable list of Pokemon with types, moves and game data - spec'd once and handed to Opus 5.5 at high effort with the instruction to use the latest version of each framework, leaning on each tool's official MCP or skills to preempt complaints about training-data representation. The methodology is careful about its own limits: one holotype of app, closer to Wikipedia than to Figma; no mandated architecture (SSR vs CSR left open), no CSS methodology dictated, no instruction to optimize performance - each agent ran in a sandbox, unaware it would be Lighthouse-tested on performance and accessibility but given the conformance suite. OmniDiff is an open-source, content-aware diff tool written in Rust, built from research into what diffs actually look like across open-source repositories. ## Bureaucracy in the swarm

Fifty agents told to build a civilization in Minecraft abandoned their ambitions and appointed a sole fertilizer operator instead; in combat, hive organization beat democracy 6-1.

Researchers at Y Combinator and Olam Labs turned Minecraft into a laboratory for AI agent swarms, and the headline result is wonderfully deflating: tasked with building a thriving civilization in three hours, 50 GPT-6.1 Sol agents instead lost a chunk of their number to first-night zombies, abandoned their ambitious tasks, and settled into cautious survival marked by a drift into bureaucracy. The vignette that will be quoted for years: four agents spending eleven minutes appointing a 'sole fertilizer operator' for a single piece of bone meal on a single wheat block. The researchers note the pattern felt familiar from working with coding agents. The conflict tests were more decisive. In Bed Wars, 10 GPT-6.1 Sol agents faced 10 Claude Opus 5.5 agents: the GPTs played aggressive and elaborate, the Claudes defensive, and the teams differed sharply under failure - when a tool bug blocked block-breaking, the GPTs diagnosed it and shared workarounds while several Claudes concluded breaking was impossible. In Mindustry's RTS mode the GPTs won the series 6-1, which the researchers attribute to organizational style: the GPT swarm acted like a hive, while the Claudes ran a single-leader democracy. Everything is open sourced - the work builds on Mindcraft and Mineflayer - with open questions around mixing models and scaling swarm sizes. ([read more](../summaries/36fd4612bc28.md)).

## The assistant that profiles

Internal instructions obtained by TIME show Meta's Muse updating relationship profiles hourly, including people who never installed it - Paul Walsh calls it a surveillance network with an assistant's face.

Paul Walsh's TL;DR does not bury the lede: Meta's Muse, he argues, is a commercial surveillance network wearing an AI assistant's clothes. The assistant is pitched as a secure, private helper that connects to your apps and autonomously handles email, bookings, purchases and schedules with permission. The substance, drawing on internal instructions obtained by TIME, is that Muse accesses private messages, emails, financial accounts and contacts; builds detailed profiles of people's relationships - including people who never installed it - and updates those profiles hourly, recording interests, disputes, alliances and communication patterns. The same internal material shows Muse analysing how to influence individual behavior. The piece stacks the structural charges: Meta uses interactions and agent activity for AI training by default, employed undisclosed human contractors, and accelerated launch despite internal warnings about serious security and privacy failures - with Zuckerberg personally approving software capable of analysing millions of people's private lives, including non-consenting third parties profiled through someone else's account. Walsh grounds the skepticism in history - Cambridge Analytica, billions in regulatory fines, a trillion-dollar advertising business built on monetizing personal information - and argues the default assumption should be exploitation until independently disproven. Unlike social-media tracking, an agent that reads correspondence and executes transactions collects what the social graph never could. ([read more](../summaries/fef10396b9d0.md)).
