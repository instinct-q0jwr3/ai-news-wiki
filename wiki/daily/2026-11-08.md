Laplace's Scissors is a Rust, Triton and CUDA/HIP tool that edits an LLM's output matrix by rotation so that chosen answers are repaired while protected correct answers stay intact. Its commands fetch and inspect a model, probe directions with weak output discrimination, diagnose failures from expected answer strings, generate, export an edited checkpoint, and analyze scaling across model sizes. Each edit is validated against actual GPU outputs before it is accepted. The project needs a Rust toolchain, Python with Triton 3.8, and a CUDA or HIP driver, and caches models and kernels under a configurable directory. The Show HN title says it was made by a 15-year-old; the README itself does not say. ([read more](../summaries/de8470ebcf3b.md)).

A reverse-engineering write-up on gamesexplained.com explains how the computer-driven opponent in the Commodore 64 game Stunt Car Racer works. The opponent car is just a handful of numbers: distance round the track, position across the road, speed, and three wheels on springs that move only up and down. The track carries the car wherever the springs put it. The page walks through the code routines involved. Each spring pushes its wheel up harder the further it is pressed into the road and the faster it is moving, against a pull down of 40 (124 on a bend), with back wheels kept within set height limits of each other. That is why the car leans on banked bends and leaves the road over a crest, where only drag slows it. Run-ups to the jump insist on a speed of 70, and a too-slow car lands in the dip between ramps. ## The workbench

Harness and design lessons for coding agents.

Sean Goedecke argues that LLMs are poor at designing software that itself uses LLMs. They write ordinary programs comfortably, but given an AI-shaped problem they tend to get it wrong. His example is a vibe-coded memory tool meant to flag contradictory memories: an LLM could simply scan for contradictions in a batch or pairwise, yet when asked for the state of the art, the agent built a complex symbolic system that converted memories into logical atoms for a solver. That approach could not work, since no analytic method decides whether two natural-language sentences express the same proposition. The model knew this, and explained it was a 130-year-old unsolved problem once the user pushed, yet it iterated on the dead end for weeks. He has seen the same while building Autodeck, where agents kept trying to categorize cards with regex. His framing is that good design depends on the requirements you do not have, and models do not weigh those well. ([read more](../summaries/033495df1e58.md)).

A Lobsters user asks what is realistic to run locally on a Mac mini with an M5 Pro and 48GB unified memory, asking for real tokens-per-second numbers and 'waste of time' stories. Replies say Qwen3.8-27B is the best local coding model but slow, short on long-context headroom, and unsuited to parallel sessions; it suits background or overnight chores or an advisor role. Smaller models are called too weak for serious coding, 4-bit quants are the sweet spot, and gemma4-e4b works as a lossy wikipedia or smarter regex. One commenter says even 96GB is barely usable for real coding and that DeepSeek 4.1 Flash is smarter, four times faster and avoids out-of-memory issues. Another runs Qwen3.6-27B at 6-bit on MLX-LM and finds it worse than Claude but fine for routine tasks and bug scans if the model must verify bugs. ([read more](../summaries/01c61f169178.md)).

## The acceleration question

The acceleration question

A blog post argues that the AI debate wrongly offers two options: full delegation to machines or full rejection. The interesting question is how humans and machines should work together. The author uses a runner asked to train a robot to run 100 meters. In the first scenario the runner stops running and shouts instructions from the sofa; the instructions get harder to give, and he loses fitness and intuition. He maps this to an engineer who only supervises an agent and slowly loses the intuition built by solving problems. The second scenario, where runner and robot run side by side, is described as more honest but not necessarily more useful, and the post points to a third, hidden option between the extremes. His point is that using AI does not mean believing it should replace engineers, and refusing it does not mean thinking it is useless. ([read more](../summaries/e168c659e71e.md)).

## The enterprise squeeze

Goldman narrows who gets coding agents.

Goldman Sachs has restricted Anthropic's Claude Code and Microsoft's GitHub to its engineers, cutting off traders and bankers who used the tools to track client flows and model markets, per Bloomberg as relayed by The State of AI. October guidance followed a review of productivity and risk concerns. Strats, who span trading, quant research and software, keep access. The bank had barred Hong Kong bankers from Claude in April and is now a lead underwriter on Anthropic's IPO. The change reverses a July message in which CIO Marco Argenti praised 'vibe coding' by non-technical staff, who had built tools and passed them to about 12,000 engineers to improve. The risk is that thousands of people without engineering training were running software that writes and executes code on machines handling client information. The Hong Kong limit rested on location and contract terms; the new one rests on role. Goldman's in-house multi-model assistant remains available firm-wide.
