# AI: sowieso ontwrichtend

## Summary

Bert Hubert makes one careful AI prediction (in Dutch, with English and German versions): even if AI never becomes malicious or superintelligent, it will be economically disruptive anyway. He refuses the usual debate about whether ChatGPT is 'really intelligent' - that question was chewed over in the 1980s Chinese Room argument and is fodder for philosophers. Companies won't care. The model may not be intelligent, but it doesn't complain about working hours, and it is already plausibly better than the humans doing triage work badly today: screening a hundred job applications at once, flagging problem files for insurers, answering product questions after reading the manuals, giving medical advice when the GP has no time.

His sober points: companies have no legal duty to think about social consequences, so if it isn't forbidden and it saves money, it will happen - and the municipality of Rotterdam already let a mediocre system make consequential decisions. The leading work is in China and America, with Europe a minor player, which means European helpdesks get automated by US AI and an even bigger stream of private data flows to 'the cloud in America.' The technology will not get worse and innovation is not hitting a wall, so large-scale economic disruption is nearly certain. He warns against citing the Industrial Revolution as proof it always works out - only people who didn't live through it call it smooth, and it took generations to benefit the majority. His practical asks: predict which sectors get plowed under first, and consider policy such as refusing 'replaced by ChatGPT' as grounds for collective dismissal.

## Highlights

- Thesis: AI is disruptive even in simple scenarios where neither humans nor AI have bad intentions
- Whether AI is 'really intelligent' is a philosopher's question - the Chinese Room debate from the 1980s already covered it
- Companies won't care about intelligence: the model doesn't complain about working hours and often beats the humans doing the job badly now
- Near-term candidates: application screening, insurer risk-flagging, product support, stopgap medical advice
- If it isn't forbidden and saves money, companies will do it - Rotterdam's municipality already handed decisions to a mediocre system
- China and the US lead; Europe's role is modest, so its helpdesks and data flow to American clouds
- The Industrial Revolution analogy comforts only those who didn't live through it - conditions were horrific for generations
- Policy ideas: map which sectors go first (Goldman Sachs already made an overview), and consider blocking 'replaced by ChatGPT' as grounds for collective dismissal
