# Building AI for Lawyers - Jacob Lauritzen, CTO of Legora

_type: news-summary · created: 2026-11-03 · updated: 2026-11-10 · confidence: high_

`latent.space`

## Summary

The full Latent Space interview with Jacob Lauritzen, CTO of Legora, recorded just after the legal-AI company's second $550 million raise of the year - October's Series E at an $8.8 billion valuation, seven months after the previous round at $5.55 billion. The numbers earn the attention: $100M+ ARR within 18 months of launch, more than 1,800 customers, five acquisitions this year. The conversation earns it more, because Lauritzen is candid about the hardest problem in applied AI that nobody talks about: Legora cannot read a single trace of what its agents do.

Everything users upload is confidential and mostly belongs to the law firms' clients, not the firms. So the standard agent-improvement loop - read traces, find failures, fix prompts - is unavailable. Legora's answer is an evals team of three engineers and three lawyers who build RL-style simulated environments that recreate customer work: a 10,000-file data room where the agent must run due diligence and catch the planted issues. The approach had skeptics internally until one model release took a citation task from 70% to 99% accuracy with no changes on Legora's side - proof, Lauritzen argues, that the environments measure real capability.

The rest of the conversation is a tour of applied-agent pragmatics: why Legora resisted a model picker for years and finally added one; why he finds some frontier models 'too chatty' for drafting and prefers concise ones; drafting a long contract as a search problem; the million-cell Tabular Review spreadsheet competitors copied 'down to the name'; lists as the primitive that lets agents create and trigger other agents; and the billable-hour question - he thinks good junior lawyers are worth more, not less, because each one now gets much more done. His slide arguing 20 engineers would be enough forever now sits next to a 100-person team, and he still feels short-handed.
