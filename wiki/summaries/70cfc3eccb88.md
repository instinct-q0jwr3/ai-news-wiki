# The Mythology of Conscious AI

_type: news-summary · created: 2026-11-22 · updated: 2026-11-25 · confidence: high_

`hacker-news`

## Summary

Noema publishes Anil Seth's Berggruen Prize-winning essay arguing that consciousness is more likely a property of life than of computation, and that creating conscious or even conscious-seeming AI is a bad idea. Seth, a neuroscience professor at the University of Sussex, contends that identifying consciousness with intelligence and language makes people see it where it does not exist and miss it where it does, reviews cultural precedents from the Golem to Ex Machina, and criticizes computational functionalism by pointing to the biological brain's materiality. He notes that cognitive biases predispose people to overattribute consciousness to machines, and warns that granting rights to systems that merely seem conscious would hamper the ability to control or shut them down.

This is a prize-winning philosophical essay, an argued position rather than a settled scientific consensus; thinkers named in the piece itself, such as David Chalmers and Geoffrey Hinton, hold more permissive views about machine consciousness. The essay's force is in its framing and its account of biological evidence, not in new experiments, and its policy conclusion about AI rights follows from premises that remain contested within consciousness science.

## Highlights

- Berggruen Prize essay by neuroscientist Anil Seth
- Central claim: consciousness tracks life, not computation
- Overattribution of machine consciousness is explained through known biases
- Conscious-seeming AI is argued to be harmful even if never conscious
- Rights for apparently conscious systems would complicate control, he argues
- A contested philosophical position, not an empirical result
