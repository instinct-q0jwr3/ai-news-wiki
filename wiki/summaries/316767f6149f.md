## Summary

Cory Doctorow's new essay opens a century before the Turing Test: from 1915 to 1925, Thomas Edison toured America with 'tone tests' - live performers and his phonograph behind curtains, the reveal being that audiences had taken the record player for the musician. Modern listeners hear those scratchy recordings and assume old-timey audiences were credulous yokels. Doctorow's answer: they weren't deaf, they were operating on a lifetime in which the sound of a performance had always implied a performer. When two things have been inseparable for all of history, the brain treats them as one.

He names the general principle through Douglas Adams' towel gambit: a hitchhiker spotted with a towel is assumed to also have the toothbrush, compass and space suit - and is happily lent any of them. Possession of one impressive attribute implies the whole kit. The Edison tone test weaponized the same inference: fidelity in one dimension borrowed credibility for the whole.

The essay is building its AI argument from this foundation - that fluent, confident text plays the phonograph's trick on us, borrowing the credibility we grant to articulate human experts. (The full development continues past the excerpt; the framing above is what the opening establishes.) Doctorow's title, 'Separable,' is the counter-thesis: the things AI makes us conflate are, in fact, separable - and learning to separate them is the era's core literacy.

## Highlights

- Doctorow: Edison's 1915-25 'tone tests' fooled audiences not because phonographs sounded real, but because performance had always implied a performer
