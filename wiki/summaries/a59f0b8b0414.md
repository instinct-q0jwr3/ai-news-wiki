# Nikon photography contest winner loses title after scientists spot signs of AI

_type: news-summary · created: 2026-10-25 · updated: 2026-10-31 · confidence: high_

`hacker-news`

## Summary

The winner of Nikon's 16th Small World in Motion contest, one of the most prestigious scientific photography awards, was stripped of his title after researchers concluded his entry was AI-generated. Dr. Ning Xu of Tsinghua University had won with a video of cilia beating in the airways of a child with primary ciliary dyskinesia. Scientists flagged structures that 'pop in and out of existence' and cilia that 'appear from nowhere,' and a UT Southwestern PhD student found what appeared to be an AI-generation watermark in the source image; two former judges also raised concerns.

In a now-deleted LinkedIn post, Xu admitted using AI in post-processing but insisted it only helped distinguish and visualize features in reconstructed grayscale images, not to generate the video, cilia or their movement, and that he broke no rules. Skeptics noted the explanation doesn't account for the abnormalities, and that sharing the RAW grayscale video could have settled the question. Nikon's investigation found a rules violation, awarded the top prize to Vietnam's Nguyen Nam Nhat (a roundworm video), and said it will revisit rules and evaluation procedures. The piece situates the case in a years-long run of AI-generated entries winning human competitions, from the 2022 Colorado State Fair to a 2023 digiDirect photo contest.

## Highlights

- Nikon stripped the winner of its Small World in Motion contest after his entry was judged AI-generated.
- Dr. Ning Xu's winning video showed cilia in the airways of a child with primary ciliary dyskinesia.
- Scientists spotted structures popping in and out of existence and an apparent AI watermark in the source image.
- Xu admitted AI use in post-processing but denied generating the video; he has not released the RAW grayscale footage.
- Nikon awarded the prize to Nguyen Nam Nhat of Vietnam and will revisit competition rules and evaluation procedures.
- The case extends a years-long pattern of AI-generated work entering - and sometimes winning - human competitions.
