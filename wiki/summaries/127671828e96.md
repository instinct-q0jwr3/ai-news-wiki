# Unlocking hidden skills inside Google's model

_type: news-summary · created: 2026-11-02 · updated: 2026-11-11 · confidence: high_

`tldr-ai` `google`

## Summary

AI models hold skills no prompt can unlock, and it takes absurdly few trained weights to surface them. A NeurIPS 2025 study (Donoway and colleagues from Anthropic, UC Berkeley, Constellation, Thinking Machines and Scale AI) fine-tuned tiny numbers of randomly chosen weights in Llama 3 and Qwen 2.5 models from 1B to 8B across ten tasks: 10 to 100 trained weights closed up to half the gap between the base model and a fully fine-tuned one. Tilo Mitra repeated the experiment on Google's smallest Gemma, the 270M - small enough to train on one 8GB consumer GPU.

The Gemma results are vivid: the best of 20 prompt wordings got the model to sort five numbers correctly 1 percent of the time (17 of 20 prompts scored zero; the usual reply just echoes the list back). After training 1,000 of its 100 million weights - 0.001 percent - it sorted correctly 97 percent of the time. The same method switched on word-list reversal (84 percent) and clean JSON output (99 percent with just 30 weights), lifted long addition, and still failed at multiplication even with 100,000 trained weights. The implication cuts both ways: latent capability is cheap to elicit, which is good news for tinkerers and an awkward fact for anyone whose safety case rests on a skill being 'not in the model.'

## Highlights

- NeurIPS 2025: training 10-100 random weights closes up to half the base-to-fine-tuned gap
- Replication on Gemma 3 270M: sorting goes from 1% (best of 20 prompts) to 97% after training 0.001% of weights
- Word reversal 84%; clean JSON 99% with 30 weights; multiplication stubborn near 30% even at 100k weights
- Prompting cannot reach these skills - the capability is latent, not absent
- Cuts both ways: cheap elicitation for tinkerers, awkward for 'the model can't do that' safety claims
