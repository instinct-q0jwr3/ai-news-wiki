## Summary

Cory Doctorow argues that competition matters whether you love markets or fear them. Pro-market: without rivals, companies go 'too big to care.' Anti-market: without rivals, industries collapse into cartels that collude, mint cash and capture their regulators - 'too big to jail.' Either way, oligarchy is the enemy, and that lens makes Big Tech's authoritarian turn legible.

Tech should have been different: every computer is a universal machine, so every '10-foot pile of shit' a platform adds induces someone to build an 11-foot ladder - adversarial interoperability, the early-web cycle of pirates becoming admirals becoming disrupted by new pirates. What killed it wasn't technology but policy: Section 1201 of the 1998 DMCA made reverse-engineering and modification a felony punishable by $500k and five years in prison. That one-way ratchet progressively outlawed the disenshittification ecosystem, until American startups gave up on disrupting Big Tech and reoriented toward acqui-hires - being absorbed and mothballed by the incumbents they once would have dethroned.

## Highlights

- Both theories of markets need competition: it disciplines firms ('too big to care') and keeps them governable ('too big to jail')
- Oligarchic sectors stop competing, collude, and buy the policy environment that suits them
- Universal computers make tech intrinsically competitive: every enshittificatory move invites a countermeasure
- Adversarial interoperability - mods, plugins, reverse-engineered complements - powered early tech's constant ferment
- The cycle: pirates disrupt, become admirals, get disrupted by new pirates
- DMCA Section 1201 (1998) made that a felony: $500k and five years for modifying or reverse-engineering
- Result: a one-way ratchet toward lock-in; startups now aim to be acqui-hired rather than to displace incumbents
