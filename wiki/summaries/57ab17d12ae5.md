# Inside Meta's law enforcement outreach team, which uses AI and human reviewers to analyze social media posts, aiming to prevent threats like school shootings

_type: news-summary · created: 2026-10-24 · updated: 2026-10-27 · confidence: high_

`techmeme` `meta`

## Summary

The New Yorker goes inside Meta's law-enforcement-outreach team, which for a decade has been refining a method to identify potential school shooters before they act. The approach grew out of profiler Mary Ellen O'Toole's observation that most under-18 shooters 'leak' their intentions in advance - nearly nine in ten, per an FBI study of 2000-2013 attacks. Emily Vacher, a former FBI agent who leads the effort, assembled journalists, police investigators and engineers in 2018 to study past attackers' online footprints and build AI tools that scan for signals; flagged content goes to human reviewers who examine posts, interactions and even chatbot prompts, and when there is evidence of 'substantial steps, overt acts,' Meta hands information to law enforcement - about 800 times worldwide last year. In one Queens case, a student who posted a gun threat on Instagram was in custody just over two hours later, with a loaded pistol in his backpack.

The piece also maps the terrain the team polices: the True Crime Community, an online fandom celebrating mass shooters, linked in two years to at least nine attacks and sixteen disrupted plots, per the Institute for Strategic Dialogue. T.C.C. users constantly evolve coded language and imagery - nicknames like Arlene and Smiggles, childhood photos of killers, recycled iconography from each new attack - outpacing moderation tools, while trust-and-safety teams across the industry have been downsized. The story opens with the foiled plot by two Texas teenagers, uncovered via a Meta tip, and closes on the uncomfortable frame: algorithmic social media helped create the conditions for this content, and is now reckoning with being part of the solution.
